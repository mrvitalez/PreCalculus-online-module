5.1The Unit Circle

Trigonometry can be approached in two ways: through angles in right triangles (Module 6), or through real numbers on a circle. In this module we take the second road, which is the one calculus uses. Everything starts with one simple circle.

Definition The unit circle is the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin in the \(xy\)-plane. Its equation is
\[ x^2 + y^2 = 1 \qquad (1) \]

A point lies on the unit circle exactly when its coordinates satisfy equation (1). For example, is \(P\left(\dfrac{3}{5}, -\dfrac{4}{5}\right)\) on the unit circle? Let's check: \(\left(\dfrac35\right)^2 + \left(-\dfrac45\right)^2 = \dfrac{9}{25} + \dfrac{16}{25} = 1\). Yes, it is.

Terminal points. Suppose \(t\) is a real number. Let's start at the point \((1, 0)\) and walk a distance \(t\) along the unit circle: counterclockwise if \(t\) is positive, clockwise if \(t\) is negative. The point \(P(x, y)\) where we stop is called the terminal point determined by \(t\). Since the circumference of the unit circle is \(2\pi\), walking \(t = 2\pi\) brings us all the way around back to \((1, 0)\). Half of that, \(t = \pi\), takes us to \((-1, 0)\); a quarter, \(t = \pi/2\), takes us to \((0, 1)\); and \(t = 3\pi/2\) takes us to \((0, -1)\).

On the unit circle, the arc length \(t\) is exactly the angle measure in radians, so \(t = \pi\) matches \(180^\circ\), and in general \(1^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{180}\) rad. This is why we can talk about "\(30^\circ\)" and "\(\pi/6\)" in the same breath. From now on, the input \(t\) is a real number (a length), and we write it in radians.

Deriving the terminal point of \(\pi/6\) (30°). From equation (1) we can derive the values of \(x\) and \(y\) for special values of \(t\). Let's start with \(30^\circ\). Draw the radius to \(P(x, y)\) and drop the vertical segment to the \(x\)-axis, so we get a right triangle with a \(30^\circ\) angle at the origin. If we flip this triangle over the \(x\)-axis and connect it with the original one, we create a triangle with a \(60^\circ\) angle at the origin and \(60^\circ\) angles at the other two corners (Figure 5.2a). That is an equilateral triangle, and in an equilateral triangle, since the angles are equal, so are the sides. Two of its sides are radii, so all three sides equal 1. The vertical side is made of two copies of \(y\), so \(2y = 1\), and \(y = \dfrac12\). Then, with \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\) (or simply equation (1)),

\[ x^2 + \left(\tfrac12\right)^2 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; x^2 = \tfrac34 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}. \]

So the terminal point of \(t = \pi/6\) is \(\left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \dfrac12\right)\). For \(60^\circ = \pi/3\), the triangle is the same one turned over, so \(x\) and \(y\) swap: \(\left(\dfrac12, \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\).

Deriving the terminal point of \(\pi/4\) (45°). Here both acute angles of the right triangle are \(45^\circ\). Since both have the same angle, their opposite sides are also equal (Figure 5.2b). If we let the hypotenuse, which is also the radius, be \(c = 1\) and each leg be \(a\) and \(b\), then \(a = b\). From the Pythagorean theorem,

\[ c^2 = a^2 + b^2 \;\Rightarrow\; 1^2 = 2a^2 \;\Rightarrow\; a^2 = \tfrac12 \;\Rightarrow\; a = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}. \]

Since \(a = b\), the terminal point of \(\pi/4\) is \(\left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\).

Reference numbers. The other special points come from these three by symmetry. For a real number \(t\), the reference number \(\bar t\) is the shortest distance along the unit circle between the terminal point of \(t\) and the \(x\)-axis. The terminal point of \(t\) has the same coordinates as the terminal point of \(\bar t\), except for the signs, and the signs come from the quadrant (Figure 5.3). For \(t = 5\pi/6\), the terminal point is in Quadrant II and is \(\pi/6\) short of \(\pi\), so \(\bar t = \pi/6\) and the terminal point is \(\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \dfrac12\right)\). Figure 5.1 collects all sixteen special terminal points.

If \(t\) is larger than \(2\pi\) (or negative), let's first add or subtract multiples of \(2\pi\): a full trip around the circle brings us back to the same point. For example, \(t = 13\pi/3 = 4\pi + \pi/3\) has the same terminal point as \(\pi/3\).

Take note The reference number is measured to the x-axis, never to the \(y\)-axis. For \(t = 2\pi/3\) the reference number is \(\pi - \dfrac{2\pi}{3} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\), not \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3} - \dfrac{\pi}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}\). Using \(\pi/6\) would swap \(x\) and \(y\) and give the wrong point.
Key equations
\[ x^2 + y^2 = 1 \]
\[ t = \tfrac{\pi}{6} \to \left(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \tfrac12\right), \quad t = \tfrac{\pi}{4} \to \left(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right), \quad t = \tfrac{\pi}{3} \to \left(\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) \]
\[ 180^\circ = \pi \text{ rad}, \qquad t \text{ and } t + 2n\pi \text{ have the same terminal point} \]

5.2Trigonometric Functions of Real Numbers

Now that every real number \(t\) gives a point \(P(x, y)\) on the unit circle, we can define six functions of \(t\) using the two coordinates of that point.

Definition Let \(t\) be a real number and let \(P(x, y)\) be its terminal point on the unit circle. Then
\[ \sin t = y, \qquad \cos t = x, \qquad \tan t = \frac{y}{x}\;(x \ne 0) \]
\[ \csc t = \frac{1}{y}\;(y \ne 0), \qquad \sec t = \frac{1}{x}\;(x \ne 0), \qquad \cot t = \frac{x}{y}\;(y \ne 0) \]

So sine is the \(y\)-coordinate and cosine is the \(x\)-coordinate. The other four are built from these two. Putting the terminal points from Section 5.1 into the definitions gives the summary table below.

Domains. Sine and cosine are defined for every real number. Tangent and secant divide by \(x\), so they are undefined where \(x = 0\), that is at \(t = \dfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi\). Cotangent and cosecant divide by \(y\), so they are undefined at \(t = n\pi\) (\(n\) any integer).

Signs. The sign of each function depends only on the quadrant of the terminal point (Figure 5.3b). In Quadrant I all six are positive; in II only sine and cosecant (since \(y > 0\), \(x < 0\)); in III only tangent and cotangent (both coordinates negative, so their ratio is positive); in IV only cosine and secant. A popular memory aid is "All Students Take Calculus".

Even-odd properties. The terminal point of \(-t\) is the reflection of the terminal point of \(t\) in the \(x\)-axis: \((x, y)\) becomes \((x, -y)\). So \(\cos(-t) = \cos t\) (cosine is even) while \(\sin(-t) = -\sin t\) (sine is odd). From these, tangent, cotangent and cosecant are odd and secant is even. For example, \(\cos\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac12\) and \(\sin\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{6}\right) = -\dfrac12\).

The Pythagorean identity. Since the terminal point \((\cos t, \sin t)\) is on the unit circle, equation (1) gives us, for every \(t\),

\[ \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1. \]

This lets us find one function from another. If \(\sin t = \dfrac{5}{13}\) and the terminal point is in Quadrant II, then \(\cos^2 t = 1 - \dfrac{25}{169} = \dfrac{144}{169}\), and since \(x < 0\) in Quadrant II, \(\cos t = -\dfrac{12}{13}\). The full family of identities is the topic of Module 6.

Take note \(\sin^2 t\) means \((\sin t)^2\), not \(\sin(t^2)\). And when we take a square root in \(\cos t = \pm\sqrt{1 - \sin^2 t}\), the sign is not automatically positive: it is decided by the quadrant.
Key equations
\[ \sin(-t) = -\sin t, \qquad \cos(-t) = \cos t, \qquad \tan(-t) = -\tan t \]
\[ \sin(t + 2\pi) = \sin t, \qquad \cos(t + 2\pi) = \cos t, \qquad \tan(t + \pi) = \tan t \]
\[ \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1 \]

5.3Trigonometric Graphs

As \(t\) increases from 0 to \(2\pi\), the terminal point travels once around the unit circle. Its \(y\)-coordinate (sine) climbs from 0 up to 1 at \(\pi/2\), comes back to 0 at \(\pi\), drops to \(-1\) at \(3\pi/2\) and returns to 0 at \(2\pi\). Its \(x\)-coordinate (cosine) starts at 1, reaches \(-1\) at \(\pi\) and returns to 1. After that, the point simply goes around again, so the pattern repeats (Figure 5.4).

Definition A function \(f\) is periodic if there is a positive number \(p\) such that \(f(t + p) = f(t)\) for every \(t\). The smallest such \(p\) is the period. Sine and cosine have period \(2\pi\); tangent and cotangent have period \(\pi\).

Stretching: \(y = a\sin bx\) and \(y = a\cos bx\). In graphing sine and cosine there are a few characteristics that we need to look into. Consider

\[ y = a\sin bx, \qquad y = a\cos bx \qquad (2) \]

where \(a\) and \(b\) are constants (\(b > 0\)). The constant \(a\) stretches the curve vertically: the graph now goes up to \(|a|\) and down to \(-|a|\), and \(|a|\) is called the amplitude. The constant \(b\) stretches the curve horizontally: \(bx\) runs from 0 to \(2\pi\) while \(x\) runs from 0 to \(2\pi/b\), so the period is \(2\pi/b\). A big \(b\) squeezes the waves together; a small \(b\) spreads them out (Figure 5.5). For example, \(y = 3\cos 4x\) has amplitude 3 and period \(\dfrac{2\pi}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\). If \(a\) is negative, the curve is also reflected in the \(x\)-axis.

Shifting: phase shift. A sine curve that does not start at the origin can be written as

\[ y = a\sin\big(b(x - c)\big), \qquad y = a\cos\big(b(x - c)\big) \]

(SRW writes this as \(y = a\sin k(x - b)\)). The number \(c\) is the phase shift: the graph of \(y = a\sin bx\) is moved \(c\) units right (left if \(c < 0\)). One complete period runs over the interval \(\left[c,\; c + \dfrac{2\pi}{b}\right]\). Before reading the phase shift, we must factor \(b\) out: \(y = \sin(2x - \pi) = \sin\left(2\left(x - \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\right)\) has phase shift \(\pi/2\), not \(\pi\). Adding a constant \(d\) outside, \(y = a\sin(b(x - c)) + d\), moves the whole curve up \(d\) units; the curve then oscillates between \(d - |a|\) and \(d + |a|\).

Tangent and cotangent. Since \(\tan t = \sin t/\cos t\), the tangent is 0 where \(\sin t = 0\) and has a vertical asymptote wherever \(\cos t = 0\), at \(t = \dfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi\). Between two asymptotes it rises from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\); its range is all real numbers and its period is \(\pi\). The cotangent \(\cot t = \cos t/\sin t\) has asymptotes where \(\sin t = 0\), at \(t = n\pi\), and it falls between them (Figure 5.6). For \(y = a\tan bx\) and \(y = a\cot bx\) the period is \(\pi/b\).

Secant and cosecant. The cosecant is the reciprocal of sine. Where \(\sin x = \pm 1\), \(\csc x = \pm 1\); where \(\sin x\) is close to 0, \(\csc x\) is huge; and where \(\sin x = 0\) there is an asymptote. So the graph of \(y = \csc x\) is a chain of U-shaped branches, opening up above the arches of the sine curve and down below its valleys (Figure 5.7). The same happens for \(\sec x = 1/\cos x\). Both have period \(2\pi\) and range \((-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty)\). A quick way to sketch \(y = a\csc bx\) is to first sketch \(y = a\sin bx\) lightly and then draw the branches on it.

Take note The period of \(y = \sin bx\) is \(2\pi/b\), not \(2\pi b\). And the period of \(y = \tan bx\) is \(\pi/b\), not \(2\pi/b\): tangent repeats every \(\pi\), not every \(2\pi\). Also, amplitude only makes sense for sine and cosine; \(\tan x\), \(\sec x\) and \(\csc x\) have no maximum value.
Key equations
\[ y = a\sin\big(b(x - c)\big) + d: \quad \text{amplitude } |a|, \quad \text{period } \frac{2\pi}{b}, \quad \text{phase shift } c \]
\[ y = a\tan bx, \; y = a\cot bx: \quad \text{period } \frac{\pi}{b} \]
\[ \tan x \text{ and } \sec x: \text{ asymptotes at } x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} + n\pi; \qquad \cot x \text{ and } \csc x: \text{ asymptotes at } x = n\pi \]

5.4Inverse Trigonometric Functions and Their Graphs

Suppose we know that \(\sin t = \dfrac12\). What is \(t\)? It could be \(\pi/6\), or \(5\pi/6\), or \(\pi/6 + 2\pi\), and so on: infinitely many answers. A function can only have an inverse if it is one-to-one, and the sine is far from one-to-one. The fix is to restrict the domain to a piece where the function takes each of its values exactly once, and then invert that piece.

Definition Inverse sine. Restrict sine to \(\left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]\). For \(-1 \le x \le 1\),
\[ \sin^{-1} x = y \iff \sin y = x \text{ and } -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \tfrac{\pi}{2} \]
Inverse cosine. Restrict cosine to \([0, \pi]\). For \(-1 \le x \le 1\),
\[ \cos^{-1} x = y \iff \cos y = x \text{ and } 0 \le y \le \pi \]
Inverse tangent. Restrict tangent to \(\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\). For every real \(x\),
\[ \tan^{-1} x = y \iff \tan y = x \text{ and } -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \lt y \lt \tfrac{\pi}{2} \]

The value given by an inverse trigonometric function is called the principal value. The other names are \(\arcsin x\), \(\arccos x\), \(\arctan x\) (on most calculators, the \(\sin^{-1}\) key). In words, \(\sin^{-1} x\) is "the number in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\) whose sine is \(x\)". So \(\sin^{-1}\dfrac12 = \dfrac{\pi}{6}\), the only one of the infinitely many answers above that lies in the restricted interval.

Why these intervals? For sine and tangent, the restricted interval is centered at 0, covers every output once, and contains the "nice" increasing piece. For cosine, an interval centered at 0 would not work (cosine is even, so \(\cos(-t) = \cos t\) repeats values), so we use \([0, \pi]\), where cosine decreases from 1 to \(-1\). This is why \(\cos^{-1}\) of a negative number lands in Quadrant II: \(\cos^{-1}\left(-\dfrac12\right) = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\), while \(\sin^{-1}\) of a negative number is negative: \(\sin^{-1}\left(-\dfrac12\right) = -\dfrac{\pi}{6}\).

Cancellation properties. Since \(\sin^{-1}\) undoes the restricted sine,

\[ \sin(\sin^{-1} x) = x \text{ for } -1 \le x \le 1, \qquad \sin^{-1}(\sin x) = x \text{ for } -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le x \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}. \]

The second one has a condition, and it matters: \(\sin^{-1}\left(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \sin^{-1}\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\), not \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\). Similar statements hold for cosine (with \(0 \le x \le \pi\)) and tangent (with \(-\pi/2 \lt x \lt \pi/2\)). We can also combine an inverse function with the Pythagorean identity: if \(\theta = \sin^{-1}\dfrac{3}{5}\), then \(\theta\) is in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\) where cosine is not negative, so \(\cos\left(\sin^{-1}\dfrac35\right) = \sqrt{1 - \dfrac{9}{25}} = \dfrac45\).

Take note The \(-1\) in \(\sin^{-1} x\) is not an exponent: \(\sin^{-1} x \ne \dfrac{1}{\sin x}\). The reciprocal of sine is \(\csc x = (\sin x)^{-1}\). Also, \(\sin^{-1} 2\) and \(\cos^{-1}(-1.5)\) do not exist, because no real number has a sine of 2.
Key equations
\[ \sin^{-1}: [-1, 1] \to \left[-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right], \quad \cos^{-1}: [-1, 1] \to [0, \pi], \quad \tan^{-1}: \mathbb{R} \to \left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) \]
\[ \cos(\cos^{-1} x) = x \;(-1 \le x \le 1), \qquad \cos^{-1}(\cos x) = x \;(0 \le x \le \pi) \]

5.5Modeling Harmonic Motion

Many things in nature repeat: a mass bouncing on a spring, the tide rising and falling at Manila Bay, the voltage in our wall outlets, the light of a pulsating star. Because sine and cosine are periodic, they are the natural models for this kind of behavior.

Definition An object is in simple harmonic motion if its displacement \(y\) at time \(t\) is
\[ y = a\sin\omega t \qquad \text{or} \qquad y = a\cos\omega t \quad (\omega \gt 0) \]
Its amplitude is \(|a|\) (the maximum displacement), its period is \(\dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}\) (the time for one complete cycle), and its frequency is \(\nu = \dfrac{\omega}{2\pi}\) (the number of cycles per unit time).

Notice that frequency and period are reciprocals: a pendulum with period 0.5 s makes 2 swings per second. When frequency is measured in cycles per second, the unit is the hertz (Hz). For example, household electricity in the Philippines alternates at 60 Hz, so \(\omega = 2\pi(60) = 120\pi\) and one cycle takes \(\dfrac{1}{60}\) s.

Sine or cosine? Use \(y = a\cos\omega t\) when the motion starts at its maximum displacement (for example, a spring stretched and then released), and \(y = a\sin\omega t\) when it starts at the rest position and moves up (for example, a mass given a push at equilibrium). If the motion starts at some other moment, we can add a phase shift, \(y = a\sin(\omega(t - c))\), and a vertical shift \(d\) if the motion is centered at \(y = d\) instead of 0.

A pulsating star. Cepheid variable stars swell and shrink, so their brightness rises and falls with a very regular period. Astronomers measure brightness in magnitudes, where a smaller magnitude means a brighter star. The Cepheid Eta Aquilae varies between about magnitude 3.5 and 4.4 with a period of about 7.2 days. A simplified model (Figure 5.9a), taking \(t = 0\) at maximum brightness, is \(m(t) = 3.95 - 0.45\cos\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{7.2}t\right)\): the center is the average \(\dfrac{3.5 + 4.4}{2} = 3.95\), the amplitude is \(\dfrac{4.4 - 3.5}{2} = 0.45\), and \(\omega = 2\pi/7.2\). The period-brightness relation of Cepheids is what lets astronomers measure distances to other galaxies, so this little cosine model is a doorway to cosmology.

Damped harmonic motion. In real life, friction slowly takes energy away: a guitar string fades, a car's shock absorbers stop the bouncing after a hump. The amplitude then decreases with time. A common model is

\[ y = k e^{-ct}\sin\omega t \qquad \text{or} \qquad y = k e^{-ct}\cos\omega t \quad (c \gt 0) \]

where \(c\) is the damping constant. The factor \(k e^{-ct}\) is a shrinking amplitude: the graph wiggles between the curves \(y = k e^{-ct}\) and \(y = -k e^{-ct}\) (Figure 5.9b). The frequency is still \(\omega/2\pi\).

Take note Do not confuse \(\omega\) with the frequency. In \(y = 5\sin 6\pi t\), \(\omega = 6\pi\), but the frequency is \(\dfrac{6\pi}{2\pi} = 3\) cycles per unit time and the period is \(\dfrac13\). Also, set your calculator to radian mode for these models.
Key equations
\[ y = a\sin\omega t, \quad y = a\cos\omega t: \qquad \text{amplitude } |a|, \quad \text{period } \frac{2\pi}{\omega}, \quad \text{frequency } \frac{\omega}{2\pi} \]
\[ y = k e^{-ct}\sin\omega t \quad \text{(damped motion)} \]

★Key takeaways

  • The unit circle is x² + y² = 1. A real number t determines a terminal point P(x, y) by walking a distance t from (1, 0), counterclockwise for t > 0.
  • An equilateral triangle gives the point of π/6, (√3/2, 1/2); an isosceles right triangle gives the point of π/4, (√2/2, √2/2). Every other special point comes from a reference number plus the signs of its quadrant.
  • sin t = y and cos t = x; tan t = y/x, csc t = 1/y, sec t = 1/x, cot t = x/y. Tan and sec are undefined at π/2 + nπ; cot and csc at nπ.
  • Sine is odd and cosine is even; sine and cosine repeat every 2π, tangent and cotangent every π; and sin²t + cos²t = 1 for every t.
  • For y = a sin(b(x − c)) + d: amplitude |a|, period 2π/b, phase shift c (factor out b first!), centerline y = d.
  • sin⁻¹ x lives in [−π/2, π/2], cos⁻¹ x in [0, π], tan⁻¹ x in (−π/2, π/2). These principal values are why sin⁻¹(sin x) = x only for x in [−π/2, π/2].
  • Simple harmonic motion is y = a sin ωt or a cos ωt with period 2π/ω and frequency ω/2π; damping multiplies the amplitude by e^(−ct).

Example 5.1 Points on the unit circle

(a) Show that \(P\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt5}{3}, \dfrac23\right)\) is on the unit circle. (b) The point \(Q\left(x, -\dfrac{5}{13}\right)\) is on the unit circle in Quadrant III. Find \(x\).

(SRW, The Unit Circle)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • The unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\).
  • \(P\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt5}{3}, \dfrac23\right)\); \(Q\left(x, -\dfrac{5}{13}\right)\) in Quadrant III.
  • Asked: check \(P\); find \(x\) for \(Q\).
Step 1: Substitute P into the equation

Let's square each coordinate and add.

\[\left(-\frac{\sqrt5}{3}\right)^2 + \left(\frac23\right)^2 = \frac{5}{9} + \frac{4}{9}\]

\[= \frac{9}{9} = 1\]

From this, we know that \(P\) satisfies \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\).

Step 2: Solve for x in Q

\[x^2 + \left(-\frac{5}{13}\right)^2 = 1\]

\[x^2 = 1 - \frac{25}{169} = \frac{144}{169}\]

\[x = \pm\frac{12}{13}\]

Step 3: Use the quadrant

Take note: in Quadrant III both coordinates are negative, so we take the negative root.

\[x = -\frac{12}{13}\]

Answer

(a) \(P\) is on the unit circle. (b) \(x = -\dfrac{12}{13}\), so \(Q\left(-\dfrac{12}{13}, -\dfrac{5}{13}\right)\).

Check

\(\dfrac{144}{169} + \dfrac{25}{169} = 1\), and both coordinates of \(Q\) are negative, as Quadrant III requires.

Example 5.2 Completing Activity 1: the rows for 105° and 165°

Without a calculator, find the terminal points and all six trigonometric functions of \(t = \dfrac{7\pi}{12}\) (105°) and \(t = \dfrac{11\pi}{12}\) (165°). Use the rows for \(15^\circ\) and \(75^\circ\) in Table 1.

(SRW, The Unit Circle; Trigonometric Functions of Real Numbers)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\cos\dfrac{\pi}{12} = \dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\), \(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{12} = \dfrac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\); the \(5\pi/12\) values are the same two numbers swapped.
  • Both \(7\pi/12\) and \(11\pi/12\) are in Quadrant II, where \(x \lt 0\) and \(y \gt 0\).
Step 1: Reference numbers

In Quadrant II, the reference number is the distance back to \(\pi\).

\[\bar t = \pi - \frac{7\pi}{12} = \frac{5\pi}{12} \;(75^\circ)\]

\[\bar t = \pi - \frac{11\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{12} \;(15^\circ)\]

Step 2: Terminal points (same numbers, x made negative)

\[t = \tfrac{7\pi}{12}: \quad (x, y) = \left(-\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4},\; \frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\right)\]

\[t = \tfrac{11\pi}{12}: \quad (x, y) = \left(-\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4},\; \frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\right)\]

Step 3: The other four functions for 7π/12

The magnitudes come from the \(75^\circ\) row; only the signs change. Sine and cosecant stay positive, the rest are negative.

\[\tan\tfrac{7\pi}{12} = -(2+\sqrt3), \quad \cot\tfrac{7\pi}{12} = -(2-\sqrt3) = \sqrt3 - 2\]

\[\csc\tfrac{7\pi}{12} = \sqrt6-\sqrt2, \quad \sec\tfrac{7\pi}{12} = -(\sqrt6+\sqrt2)\]

Step 4: The other four functions for 11π/12

Now we use the \(15^\circ\) row.

\[\tan\tfrac{11\pi}{12} = -(2-\sqrt3) = \sqrt3 - 2, \quad \cot\tfrac{11\pi}{12} = -(2+\sqrt3)\]

\[\csc\tfrac{11\pi}{12} = \sqrt6+\sqrt2, \quad \sec\tfrac{11\pi}{12} = -(\sqrt6-\sqrt2) = \sqrt2 - \sqrt6\]

Answer

105°: \(\sin = \frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\), \(\cos = -\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\), \(\tan = -(2+\sqrt3)\), \(\csc = \sqrt6-\sqrt2\), \(\sec = -(\sqrt6+\sqrt2)\), \(\cot = \sqrt3-2\).
165°: \(\sin = \frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\), \(\cos = -\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\), \(\tan = \sqrt3-2\), \(\csc = \sqrt6+\sqrt2\), \(\sec = \sqrt2-\sqrt6\), \(\cot = -(2+\sqrt3)\).

Check

A calculator in radian mode gives \(\cos(7\pi/12) \approx -0.2588\) and \(\tan(11\pi/12) \approx -0.2679\); also \(\sqrt3 - 2 \approx -0.2679\). The values match.

Example 5.3 Terminal points of large and negative numbers

Find the reference number and the terminal point of (a) \(t = \dfrac{29\pi}{6}\) and (b) \(t = -\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\).

(SRW, The Unit Circle)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(t = \dfrac{29\pi}{6}\) and \(t = -\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\).
  • Adding or subtracting \(2\pi\) does not change the terminal point.
Step 1: Remove full turns from 29π/6

\[\frac{29\pi}{6} - 4\pi = \frac{29\pi}{6} - \frac{24\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6}\]

So \(29\pi/6\) has the same terminal point as \(5\pi/6\), in Quadrant II.

Step 2: Reference number and point for (a)

\[\bar t = \pi - \frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{6}\]

The point of \(\pi/6\) is \(\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)\); in Quadrant II, \(x\) is negative.

\[P = \left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12\right)\]

Step 3: Add a full turn to −5π/4

\[-\frac{5\pi}{4} + 2\pi = \frac{3\pi}{4}\]

Walking \(5\pi/4\) clockwise ends in Quadrant II, the same place as \(3\pi/4\).

Step 4: Reference number and point for (b)

\[\bar t = \pi - \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4}\]

\[P = \left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}, \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\]

Answer

(a) \(\bar t = \dfrac{\pi}{6}\), \(P\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \dfrac12\right)\). (b) \(\bar t = \dfrac{\pi}{4}\), \(P\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\).

Check

Calculator (radians): \(\cos(29\pi/6) \approx -0.866 = -\sqrt3/2\) and \(\sin(-5\pi/4) \approx 0.707 = \sqrt2/2\). A common slip in (b) is to land in Quadrant III by walking counterclockwise.

Example 5.4 Six functions and the even-odd properties

(a) Find all six trigonometric functions of \(t = \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\). (b) Use the even-odd properties to find \(\cos\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\), \(\tan\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)\) and \(\csc\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{6}\right)\).

(SRW, Trigonometric Functions of Real Numbers)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(t = \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\), which is in Quadrant III (between \(\pi\) and \(3\pi/2\)).
  • Cosine and secant are even; sine, tangent, cosecant, cotangent are odd.
Step 1: Terminal point of 4π/3

\[\bar t = \frac{4\pi}{3} - \pi = \frac{\pi}{3}\]

In Quadrant III both coordinates are negative:

\[P = \left(-\frac12, -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\]

Step 2: Apply the definitions

\[\sin\tfrac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \quad \cos\tfrac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac12\]

\[\tan\tfrac{4\pi}{3} = \frac{-\sqrt3/2}{-1/2} = \sqrt3\]

\[\csc\tfrac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{2}{\sqrt3} = -\frac{2\sqrt3}{3}, \quad \sec\tfrac{4\pi}{3} = -2, \quad \cot\tfrac{4\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{3}\]

Step 3: Even-odd properties

\[\cos\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\]

\[\tan\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = -\tan\tfrac{\pi}{3} = -\sqrt3\]

\[\csc\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right) = -\csc\tfrac{\pi}{6} = -2\]

Answer

(a) \(\sin = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\), \(\cos = -\frac12\), \(\tan = \sqrt3\), \(\csc = -\frac{2\sqrt3}{3}\), \(\sec = -2\), \(\cot = \frac{\sqrt3}{3}\). (b) \(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\), \(-\sqrt3\), \(-2\).

Check

In Quadrant III only tangent and cotangent should be positive, and they are. Also \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = \frac34 + \frac14 = 1\).

Example 5.5 From one function to all six

If \(\cos t = -\dfrac{5}{13}\) and the terminal point of \(t\) is in Quadrant III, find the values of the other five trigonometric functions.

(SRW, Trigonometric Functions of Real Numbers)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\cos t = -\dfrac{5}{13}\); Quadrant III, so \(\sin t \lt 0\).
  • \(\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1\).
Step 1: Find sin t

\[\sin^2 t = 1 - \left(-\frac{5}{13}\right)^2 = 1 - \frac{25}{169}\]

\[\sin^2 t = \frac{144}{169}\]

Take note: Quadrant III means the negative root.

\[\sin t = -\frac{12}{13}\]

Step 2: Tangent and cotangent

\[\tan t = \frac{\sin t}{\cos t} = \frac{-12/13}{-5/13} = \frac{12}{5}\]

\[\cot t = \frac{5}{12}\]

Step 3: The reciprocals

\[\csc t = \frac{1}{\sin t} = -\frac{13}{12}, \qquad \sec t = \frac{1}{\cos t} = -\frac{13}{5}\]

Answer

\(\sin t = -\frac{12}{13}\), \(\tan t = \frac{12}{5}\), \(\csc t = -\frac{13}{12}\), \(\sec t = -\frac{13}{5}\), \(\cot t = \frac{5}{12}\).

Check

The terminal point is \(\left(-\frac{5}{13}, -\frac{12}{13}\right)\), and \(25 + 144 = 169\), so it is on the unit circle. Only tangent and cotangent are positive, consistent with Quadrant III.

Example 5.6 Amplitude, period and phase shift

Find the amplitude, period and phase shift of \(y = -2\sin(3x - \pi)\), state one complete period, and list its five key points.

(SRW, Trigonometric Graphs)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(y = -2\sin(3x - \pi)\).
  • Form: \(y = a\sin(b(x - c))\) with amplitude \(|a|\), period \(2\pi/b\), phase shift \(c\).
Step 1: Factor out b

\[y = -2\sin\left(3\left(x - \frac{\pi}{3}\right)\right)\]

So \(a = -2\), \(b = 3\), \(c = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\).

Step 2: Amplitude, period, phase shift

\[\text{amplitude} = |-2| = 2\]

\[\text{period} = \frac{2\pi}{3}\]

\[\text{phase shift} = \frac{\pi}{3} \text{ (to the right)}\]

Step 3: One period

\[\left[\frac{\pi}{3},\; \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{2\pi}{3}\right] = \left[\frac{\pi}{3},\; \pi\right]\]

We split this into four equal parts of length \(\dfrac{2\pi/3}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}\).

Step 4: Key points

A plain sine goes 0, max, 0, min, 0. Since \(a\) is negative, the curve is reflected: 0, min, 0, max, 0.

\[\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3}, 0\right), \left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}, -2\right), \left(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}, 0\right), \left(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}, 2\right), \left(\pi, 0\right)\]

Answer

Amplitude 2, period \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\), phase shift \(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) to the right; one period on \(\left[\dfrac{\pi}{3}, \pi\right]\) with the key points above.

Check

At \(x = \pi/2\): \(3x - \pi = \pi/2\), so \(y = -2\sin(\pi/2) = -2\). Correct. Reading the phase shift as \(\pi\) (without factoring out 3) is the usual mistake.

Example 5.7 Tangent and cosecant graphs

(a) Find the period, the vertical asymptotes and the zeros of \(y = \tan 2x\). (b) For \(y = 3\csc x\), find the asymptotes and the range.

(SRW, Trigonometric Graphs)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(y = \tan 2x\): tangent has period \(\pi\), asymptotes where the inside equals \(\frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi\).
  • \(y = 3\csc x = \dfrac{3}{\sin x}\).
Step 1: Period of tan 2x

\[\text{period} = \frac{\pi}{b} = \frac{\pi}{2}\]

Step 2: Asymptotes and zeros of tan 2x

Let's set the inside equal to where \(\tan\) is undefined:

\[2x = \frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi\]

\[x = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{n\pi}{2}\]

Zeros, where \(\tan\) is 0:

\[2x = n\pi \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{n\pi}{2}\]

Step 3: y = 3 csc x

Asymptotes where \(\sin x = 0\): \(x = n\pi\). Since \(|\sin x| \le 1\),

\[|3\csc x| = \frac{3}{|\sin x|} \ge 3\]

Answer

(a) Period \(\pi/2\); asymptotes \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{4} + \dfrac{n\pi}{2}\); zeros \(x = \dfrac{n\pi}{2}\). One period: \(-\dfrac{\pi}{4} \lt x \lt \dfrac{\pi}{4}\). (b) Asymptotes \(x = n\pi\); range \((-\infty, -3] \cup [3, \infty)\).

Check

\(\tan(2 \cdot \pi/8) = \tan(\pi/4) = 1\), a point halfway between the zero at 0 and the asymptote at \(\pi/4\), just like \(\tan x\) at \(\pi/4\). And \(3\csc(\pi/2) = 3\), the bottom of an upper branch.

Example 5.8 Principal values

Find the exact value: (a) \(\sin^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\) (b) \(\cos^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\) (c) \(\tan^{-1}(-1)\) (d) \(\cos^{-1} 2\).

(SRW, Inverse Trigonometric Functions and Their Graphs)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Ranges: \(\sin^{-1}\) in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\), \(\cos^{-1}\) in \([0, \pi]\), \(\tan^{-1}\) in \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\).
  • Domains: \(\sin^{-1}\) and \(\cos^{-1}\) accept only \([-1, 1]\).
Step 1: (a)

We need \(y\) in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\) with \(\sin y = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\). Since \(\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and sine is odd,

\[\sin^{-1}\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = -\frac{\pi}{3}\]

Step 2: (b)

We need \(y\) in \([0, \pi]\) with \(\cos y = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\): a Quadrant II number with reference \(\pi/4\).

\[\cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4}\]

Step 3: (c)

\(\tan\frac{\pi}{4} = 1\) and tangent is odd, and \(-\pi/4\) is in \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\).

\[\tan^{-1}(-1) = -\frac{\pi}{4}\]

Step 4: (d)

Take note: 2 is not in \([-1, 1]\). No real number has cosine 2.

Answer

(a) \(-\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) (b) \(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\) (c) \(-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) (d) undefined.

Check

Each answer lies in the right range, and \(\cos(3\pi/4) = -\sqrt2/2\). Writing \(\cos^{-1}(-\sqrt2/2) = -\pi/4\) is a typical error: \(-\pi/4\) is not in \([0, \pi]\).

Example 5.9 Cancellation and compositions

Evaluate (a) \(\sin^{-1}\left(\sin\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)\) (b) \(\cos^{-1}\left(\cos\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\right)\) (c) \(\tan\left(\sin^{-1}\dfrac35\right)\).

(SRW, Inverse Trigonometric Functions and Their Graphs)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\sin^{-1}(\sin x) = x\) only for \(x\) in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\); \(\cos^{-1}(\cos x) = x\) only for \(x\) in \([0, \pi]\).
  • \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\).
Step 1: (a) Work from the inside out

\(5\pi/6\) is not in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\), so we cannot cancel. Let's evaluate the inside first.

\[\sin\frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac12\]

\[\sin^{-1}\frac12 = \frac{\pi}{6}\]

Step 2: (b)

\(-\pi/4\) is not in \([0, \pi]\). Cosine is even, so

\[\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\]

\[\cos^{-1}\frac{\sqrt2}{2} = \frac{\pi}{4}\]

Step 3: (c) Name the inside

Let \(\theta = \sin^{-1}\frac35\). Then \(\sin\theta = \frac35\) and \(\theta\) is in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\), where \(\cos\theta \ge 0\).

\[\cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{9}{25}} = \frac45\]

\[\tan\theta = \frac{3/5}{4/5} = \frac34\]

Answer

(a) \(\dfrac{\pi}{6}\) (b) \(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) (c) \(\dfrac34\).

Check

Calculator (radians): \(\sin^{-1}(\sin 2.618) \approx 0.5236 = \pi/6\), and \(\tan(\sin^{-1} 0.6) = \tan(0.6435) \approx 0.75\).

Example 5.10 A vibrating spring

A mass hanging from a spring is pulled 6 cm below its rest position and released. It makes 3 complete oscillations every second. Taking upward as positive, (a) write a model for its displacement \(y\) (in cm) after \(t\) seconds, (b) find its period and amplitude, and (c) find its position at \(t = \dfrac19\) s.

(SRW, Modeling Harmonic Motion)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Starts at its lowest point, \(y(0) = -6\), so we use cosine with \(a = -6\).
  • Frequency \(\nu = 3\) Hz.
Step 1: Find ω from the frequency

\[\nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = 3\]

\[\omega = 6\pi\]

Step 2: Write the model

\[y(t) = -6\cos(6\pi t)\]

Check: \(y(0) = -6\cos 0 = -6\). Good.

Step 3: Period and amplitude

\[\text{period} = \frac{2\pi}{6\pi} = \frac13 \text{ s}, \qquad \text{amplitude} = |-6| = 6 \text{ cm}\]

Step 4: Position at t = 1/9

\[y\left(\tfrac19\right) = -6\cos\left(\frac{6\pi}{9}\right) = -6\cos\frac{2\pi}{3}\]

\[= -6\left(-\frac12\right) = 3\]

Answer

(a) \(y = -6\cos 6\pi t\). (b) Period \(\frac13\) s, amplitude 6 cm. (c) At \(t = \frac19\) s the mass is 3 cm above its rest position.

Check

\(1/9\) s is one third of a period. In one third of a cycle the mass has passed the rest position (at \(1/4\) of a period) and is on its way up, so a positive value makes sense.

Example 5.11 When does a Cepheid dim to magnitude 4.1?

Using the model \(m(t) = 3.95 - 0.45\cos\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{7.2}t\right)\) for the Cepheid Eta Aquilae (\(t\) in days, \(t = 0\) at maximum brightness), (a) find its magnitude 2 days after maximum, and (b) find the first time after maximum when its magnitude is 4.1.

(SRW, Modeling Harmonic Motion; Inverse Trigonometric Functions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(m(t) = 3.95 - 0.45\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{7.2}t\right)\); a larger magnitude means a dimmer star.
  • Calculator in radian mode.
Step 1: (a) Substitute t = 2

\[m(2) = 3.95 - 0.45\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{7.2}\right)\]

\[= 3.95 - 0.45\cos(1.7453) = 3.95 - 0.45(-0.1736)\]

\[\approx 4.03\]

Step 2: (b) Set m(t) = 4.1 and isolate the cosine

\[3.95 - 0.45\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{7.2}t\right) = 4.1\]

\[-0.45\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{7.2}t\right) = 0.15\]

\[\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{7.2}t\right) = -\frac13\]

Step 3: Use the inverse cosine

The star dims during the first half period, while \(\frac{2\pi}{7.2}t\) runs from 0 to \(\pi\). That is exactly the range of \(\cos^{-1}\), so

\[\frac{2\pi}{7.2}t = \cos^{-1}\left(-\frac13\right) \approx 1.9106\]

\[t \approx \frac{7.2(1.9106)}{2\pi} \approx 2.19\]

Answer

(a) About magnitude 4.03. (b) About 2.19 days after maximum brightness.

Check

\(m(2.19) = 3.95 - 0.45\cos(1.911) \approx 3.95 + 0.15 = 4.10\). It also fits (a): at 2 days the magnitude 4.03 is still a bit below 4.1.

Example 5.12 A damped seismometer needle

After a small tremor, the needle of a PHIVOLCS seismometer moves according to \(y = 8e^{-0.4t}\sin 2\pi t\), where \(y\) is in millimetres and \(t\) in seconds (Figure 5.9b). (a) Find the frequency of the vibration. (b) Find the amplitude factor \(8e^{-0.4t}\) at \(t = 3\) s. (c) When does this amplitude drop to 1 mm?

(SRW, Modeling Harmonic Motion: damped motion)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(y = k e^{-ct}\sin\omega t\) with \(k = 8\), \(c = 0.4\), \(\omega = 2\pi\).
Step 1: (a) Frequency

\[\nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{2\pi}{2\pi} = 1 \text{ Hz}\]

Step 2: (b) Amplitude at t = 3

\[8e^{-0.4(3)} = 8e^{-1.2}\]

\[\approx 8(0.3012) \approx 2.41 \text{ mm}\]

Step 3: (c) Solve 8e^(−0.4t) = 1

\[e^{-0.4t} = \frac18\]

\[-0.4t = \ln\frac18 = -\ln 8\]

\[t = \frac{\ln 8}{0.4} \approx 5.20 \text{ s}\]

Answer

(a) 1 Hz (one vibration per second). (b) About 2.41 mm. (c) After about 5.2 seconds.

Check

In Figure 5.9b, the dashed envelope is near 2.4 at \(t = 3\) and near 1 at \(t = 5.2\). Notice the frequency does not change as the motion dies away; only the amplitude shrinks.
