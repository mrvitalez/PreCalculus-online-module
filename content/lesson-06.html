6.1Angle Measure

Trigonometry literally means "triangle measurement". The ancient astronomers built it to measure things they could not reach: the size of the Earth, the distance to the Moon, the positions of stars. Everything starts with an angle. An angle \(AOB\) is made by two rays with a common vertex \(O\). We think of it as a rotation: the initial side \(OA\) turns to the terminal side \(OB\). A counterclockwise rotation gives a positive angle, and a clockwise rotation gives a negative one.

Degrees. One full turn is \(360°\). This is the unit you already know, and it comes to us from Babylonian astronomers. It is convenient for surveying and navigation, but it is an arbitrary choice. Why 360 and not 100?

Radians. Mathematics prefers a unit that comes from the circle itself. Draw a circle of radius \(r\) with its centre at the vertex of the angle. If the angle cuts off an arc that is exactly as long as the radius, the angle measures 1 radian. A full circle has circumference \(2\pi r\), so a full turn is \(2\pi\) radians. From this, we know that \(360° = 2\pi\) rad, or simply \(180° = \pi\) rad.

Definition If a circle of radius \(r\) is drawn with its centre at the vertex of an angle, the radian measure of the angle is the length of the arc it cuts off divided by \(r\). Since \(180° = \pi\) rad:
\[ 1° = \frac{\pi}{180}\ \text{rad}, \qquad 1\ \text{rad} = \frac{180°}{\pi} \approx 57.296° \]

So, to change degrees to radians we multiply by \(\dfrac{\pi}{180}\), and to change radians to degrees we multiply by \(\dfrac{180}{\pi}\). For example, \(60° = 60 \cdot \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\) rad, and \(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\) rad \(= \dfrac{3\pi}{4} \cdot \dfrac{180°}{\pi} = 135°\). When no unit is written, the angle is in radians. Figure 6.2 shows the angles we will use again and again, in both units. Try to memorise them.

Standard position and coterminal angles. An angle is in standard position when its vertex is at the origin and its initial side lies on the positive \(x\)-axis. Two angles in standard position are coterminal if their terminal sides coincide. Since one full turn brings us back to the same ray, we get coterminal angles by adding or subtracting multiples of \(360°\) (or \(2\pi\)). For instance, \(30°\), \(390°\) and \(-330°\) are all coterminal.

Arc length and area of a sector. Here is the real reason we like radians. In a circle of radius \(r\), a central angle of \(\theta\) radians cuts off an arc of length \(s = r\theta\) and a sector of area \(A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta\) (Figure 6.1). Both formulas follow from proportion: the arc is the fraction \(\dfrac{\theta}{2\pi}\) of the circumference \(2\pi r\), and the sector is the same fraction of the area \(\pi r^2\).

Let's try one. On a circle of radius 10 cm, a central angle of \(72°\) is \(72 \cdot \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{2\pi}{5}\) rad. The arc is \(s = 10 \cdot \dfrac{2\pi}{5} = 4\pi \approx 12.57\) cm, and the sector has area \(A = \tfrac12(10)^2 \cdot \dfrac{2\pi}{5} = 20\pi \approx 62.83\) cm².

Angular size in astronomy. Astronomers use \(s = r\theta\) every night. When an object of diameter \(D\) is very far away, at distance \(r\), its diameter is almost the same as a tiny arc of a huge circle centred at our eye. So its angular size is \(\theta \approx \dfrac{D}{r}\) radians. The Sun has diameter about \(1.39 \times 10^6\) km and is about \(1.496 \times 10^8\) km away, so \(\theta \approx 0.00929\) rad \(\approx 0.53°\). Take note that we must convert to degrees only at the end; the formula itself works in radians.

Linear and angular speed. Suppose a point moves along a circle of radius \(r\) and, in time \(t\), the radius sweeps an angle \(\theta\) while the point travels the arc \(s\). The angular speed is \(\omega = \theta/t\) and the linear speed is \(v = s/t\). Since \(s = r\theta\), we get \(v = r\omega\). A jeepney wheel of radius 0.35 m turning at 8 revolutions per second has \(\omega = 8 \cdot 2\pi = 16\pi \approx 50.27\) rad/s, so the jeepney moves at \(v = 0.35 \cdot 16\pi \approx 17.59\) m/s, about 63 km/h.

Key equations
\[ \text{degrees} \to \text{radians: } \times\frac{\pi}{180}, \qquad \text{radians} \to \text{degrees: } \times\frac{180}{\pi} \]
\[ s = r\theta, \qquad A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta \qquad (\theta \text{ in radians}) \]
\[ \omega = \frac{\theta}{t}, \qquad v = \frac{s}{t} = r\omega \]
Take note The formulas \(s = r\theta\) and \(A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta\) are true only when \(\theta\) is in radians. A very common mistake is to plug in degrees: with \(r = 10\) and \(\theta = 72°\), "\(s = 10 \cdot 72 = 720\) cm" is longer than the whole circle (about 62.8 cm)! Convert first. Likewise, in \(v = r\omega\), the angular speed must be in radians per unit time, not revolutions per unit time.

6.2Trigonometry of Right Triangles

Now let's put an angle inside a right triangle. Take an acute angle \(\theta\) of a right triangle. Relative to \(\theta\), the three sides have names: the hypotenuse (opposite the right angle, the longest side), the opposite side (across from \(\theta\)) and the adjacent side (the leg touching \(\theta\)). See Figure 6.3.

The key fact is that similar triangles have equal ratios of sides. Any two right triangles with the same acute angle \(\theta\) are similar, so a ratio like opp/hyp depends only on \(\theta\), not on the size of the triangle. That is why these ratios deserve names.

Definition For an acute angle \(\theta\) of a right triangle, the six trigonometric ratios are
\[ \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}} \]
\[ \csc\theta = \frac{\text{hyp}}{\text{opp}}, \qquad \sec\theta = \frac{\text{hyp}}{\text{adj}}, \qquad \cot\theta = \frac{\text{adj}}{\text{opp}} \]
Memory aid: SOH-CAH-TOA (Sine = Opp/Hyp, Cosine = Adj/Hyp, Tangent = Opp/Adj). The last three are the reciprocals of the first three.

For example, in a right triangle with legs 8 and 15, the hypotenuse is \(\sqrt{64 + 225} = 17\). If \(\theta\) is opposite the leg 8, then \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{8}{17}\), \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{15}{17}\), \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{8}{15}\), and flipping each, \(\csc\theta = \dfrac{17}{8}\), \(\sec\theta = \dfrac{17}{15}\), \(\cot\theta = \dfrac{15}{8}\).

Special triangles. For three angles we can find the exact ratios without a calculator (Figure 6.4). Cut an equilateral triangle of side 2 in half: we get a 30°-60°-90° triangle with hypotenuse 2, short leg 1 (opposite 30°) and, by the Pythagorean Theorem, long leg \(\sqrt{2^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt3\). Cut a square of side 1 along its diagonal: we get a 45°-45°-90° triangle with legs 1 and 1 and hypotenuse \(\sqrt2\).

Key equations (special angles)
\[ \sin 30° = \tfrac12, \quad \cos 30° = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \quad \tan 30° = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{3} \]
\[ \sin 45° = \cos 45° = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \quad \tan 45° = 1 \]
\[ \sin 60° = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \quad \cos 60° = \tfrac12, \quad \tan 60° = \sqrt3 \]

Do you see the pattern? \(\sin 30° = \cos 60°\) and \(\sin 60° = \cos 30°\). The two acute angles of a right triangle add up to \(90°\), and the side opposite one is adjacent to the other. So \(\sin\theta = \cos(90° - \theta)\): this is where the "co" in cosine comes from.

Solving a right triangle means finding all its sides and angles. We need either two sides, or one side and one acute angle. Let's say the hypotenuse is 10 and one angle is \(30°\). Then the side opposite \(30°\) is \(10\sin 30° = 5\), the other leg is \(10\cos 30° = 5\sqrt3 \approx 8.66\), and the other angle is \(90° - 30° = 60°\).

Angles of elevation and depression. If you look up at an object, the angle between your line of sight and the horizontal is the angle of elevation. If you look down, it is the angle of depression (Figure 6.5). This is how a surveyor measures the height of a building in Manila without climbing it: she measures her distance to the base and the angle of elevation of the top, and then height = distance \(\times \tan(\text{angle})\).

Take note Always name opp and adj relative to the angle you are using. The side opposite \(30°\) is adjacent to \(60°\). Also, an angle of depression is measured from the horizontal, not from the vertical wall of the building. Finally, check your calculator is in degree mode when the angle is in degrees: \(\sin 30 \approx -0.988\) in radian mode!

6.3Trigonometric Functions of Angles

A right triangle only has acute angles. But angles in astronomy and navigation can be \(150°\), \(300°\) or even negative. So let's extend the definitions to any angle. Put \(\theta\) in standard position and choose any point \(P(x, y)\), other than the origin, on its terminal side. Let \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) be the distance from the origin to \(P\) (so \(r \gt 0\)). See Figure 6.6.

Definition Let \(\theta\) be an angle in standard position and \(P(x, y)\) a point on its terminal side, with \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\). Then
\[ \sin\theta = \frac{y}{r}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{x}{r}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} \]
\[ \csc\theta = \frac{r}{y}, \qquad \sec\theta = \frac{r}{x}, \qquad \cot\theta = \frac{x}{y} \]
(a function is undefined when its denominator is 0).

When \(\theta\) is acute, \(P\) is in the first quadrant and \(x\), \(y\), \(r\) are just adj, opp and hyp, so nothing changes. The new thing is that \(x\) or \(y\) may now be negative. For example, if the terminal side passes through \(P(-3, 4)\), then \(r = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\), so \(\sin\theta = \dfrac45\), \(\cos\theta = -\dfrac35\) and \(\tan\theta = -\dfrac43\).

Signs by quadrant. Since \(r \gt 0\), the sign of each function depends only on the signs of \(x\) and \(y\). In Quadrant I everything is positive; in II only sine (and cosecant) is positive, since \(y \gt 0\) but \(x \lt 0\); in III only tangent (and cotangent), since \(x\) and \(y\) are both negative; in IV only cosine (and secant). Remember it as ASTC, "All Students Take Calculus".

Reference angles. The reference angle \(\bar\theta\) of \(\theta\) is the acute angle formed by the terminal side and the \(x\)-axis. The values of the trig functions at \(\theta\) are the same as at \(\bar\theta\), except possibly for sign. This gives a three-step recipe: (1) find \(\bar\theta\), (2) find the value at \(\bar\theta\), (3) attach the sign from the quadrant. For example, \(150°\) is in Quadrant II with \(\bar\theta = 180° - 150° = 30°\); sine is positive there, so \(\sin 150° = \sin 30° = \tfrac12\), while \(\cos 150° = -\cos 30° = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\).

Key equations (reference angle \(\bar\theta\) for \(0° \lt \theta \lt 360°\))
\[ \text{QII: } \bar\theta = 180° - \theta, \qquad \text{QIII: } \bar\theta = \theta - 180°, \qquad \text{QIV: } \bar\theta = 360° - \theta \]

The fundamental identities. Directly from the definitions, the reciprocal identities hold. And dividing \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) by \(r^2\) gives \(\left(\dfrac{x}{r}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{y}{r}\right)^2 = 1\), which is the first Pythagorean identity. Dividing it by \(\cos^2\theta\) or by \(\sin^2\theta\) gives the other two.

Fundamental identities
\[ \csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}, \quad \sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}, \quad \cot\theta = \frac{1}{\tan\theta}, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}, \quad \cot\theta = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} \]
\[ \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1, \qquad \tan^2\theta + 1 = \sec^2\theta, \qquad 1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta \]

With these identities, one trig value and the quadrant determine all the others. If \(\cos\theta = \tfrac{5}{13}\) and \(\theta\) is in Quadrant IV, then \(\sin^2\theta = 1 - \tfrac{25}{169} = \tfrac{144}{169}\), and since sine is negative in Quadrant IV, \(\sin\theta = -\tfrac{12}{13}\).

Area of a triangle. Here is a nice use of sine. In a triangle with sides \(a\) and \(b\) and included angle \(C\), the height to side \(a\) is \(h = b\sin C\). So the area is \(\tfrac12 \cdot a \cdot h = \tfrac12 ab\sin C\). For example, a triangle with sides 6 and 8 and included angle \(150°\) has area \(\tfrac12(6)(8)\sin 150° = 24 \cdot \tfrac12 = 12\).

Key equations
\[ \text{Area} = \tfrac12 ab\sin C \]
Take note The reference angle is always measured to the \(x\)-axis, never to the \(y\)-axis: the reference angle of \(120°\) is \(60°\), not \(30°\). And do not forget the sign. A classic slip is \(\sin 210° = \tfrac12\); in Quadrant III sine is negative, so \(\sin 210° = -\tfrac12\). Also note that when you take a square root in \(\sin\theta = \pm\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\theta}\), the quadrant decides the sign.

6.4Inverse Trigonometric Functions and Right Triangles

So far we used an angle to find a ratio. In real problems we often go backwards: we know two sides and want the angle. A ramp rises 1.2 m over a horizontal distance of 10 m. How steep is it? We know \(\tan\theta = 0.12\), and we want \(\theta\). This calls for an inverse trigonometric function.

The trig functions are not one-to-one (many angles have the same sine), so to define an inverse we restrict each function to an interval where it is one-to-one. The inverse then returns the one angle in that interval.

Definition
\[ \sin^{-1}x = y \iff \sin y = x \quad \text{and} \quad -90° \le y \le 90° \;\; \left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right),\; -1 \le x \le 1 \]
\[ \cos^{-1}x = y \iff \cos y = x \quad \text{and} \quad 0° \le y \le 180° \;\; (0 \le y \le \pi),\; -1 \le x \le 1 \]
\[ \tan^{-1}x = y \iff \tan y = x \quad \text{and} \quad -90° \lt y \lt 90° \;\; \left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \lt y \lt \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) \]
They are also written \(\arcsin x\), \(\arccos x\) and \(\arctan x\).

For an acute angle of a right triangle, all three restricted ranges contain \(0°\) to \(90°\), so the inverse functions give exactly the angle we want. For the ramp, \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.12) \approx 6.84°\). Another one: a 10 m guy wire is attached to the top of a 7 m pole. The angle the wire makes with the ground satisfies \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{7}{10}\), so \(\theta = \sin^{-1}(0.7) \approx 44.43°\). For the special values, no calculator is needed: \(\sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = 30°\), \(\cos^{-1}\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} = 45°\), \(\tan^{-1}\sqrt3 = 60°\).

Compositions. What is \(\cos(\sin^{-1} x)\)? Let's give the inner angle a name: \(\theta = \sin^{-1} x\), so \(\sin\theta = x = \dfrac{x}{1}\). Draw a right triangle with opposite side \(x\) and hypotenuse 1 (Figure 6.7). By the Pythagorean Theorem, the adjacent side is \(\sqrt{1 - x^2}\). Then we just read off \(\cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - x^2}\). The same triangle gives \(\tan(\sin^{-1}x) = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}\). For a number, like \(\tan\left(\cos^{-1}\tfrac45\right)\), we draw adj 4 and hyp 5, so opp \(= 3\) and the answer is \(\tfrac34\).

Key equations
\[ \theta = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}\right) = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}\right) = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\right) \quad (\theta \text{ acute}) \]
\[ \cos(\sin^{-1}x) = \sqrt{1 - x^2}, \qquad \sin(\cos^{-1}x) = \sqrt{1 - x^2} \qquad (-1 \le x \le 1) \]
Take note \(\sin^{-1}x\) is the inverse sine, not the reciprocal: \(\sin^{-1}x \ne \dfrac{1}{\sin x}\). The reciprocal is \(\csc x\). Also, \(\sin^{-1}(\sin\theta) = \theta\) only when \(\theta\) is in \([-90°, 90°]\): for example \(\sin^{-1}(\sin 150°) = \sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = 30°\), not \(150°\).

6.5The Law of Sines

Right-triangle trigonometry is powerful, but most triangles in surveying and astronomy are oblique (they have no right angle). We label a triangle with angles \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) and the sides opposite them \(a\), \(b\), \(c\). A triangle is determined by three parts, as long as at least one is a side. The four cases are:

  • ASA or SAA: one side and two angles;
  • SSA: two sides and an angle opposite one of them;
  • SAS: two sides and the included angle;
  • SSS: three sides.

The first two are solved with the Law of Sines; the last two need the Law of Cosines (Section 6.6).

The Law of Sines In any triangle \(ABC\),
\[ \frac{\sin A}{a} = \frac{\sin B}{b} = \frac{\sin C}{c} \]
Use it when you know a side and the angle opposite it (cases ASA, SAA and SSA).

Why is it true? Drop the height \(h\) from \(C\) to side \(c\). From the two right triangles, \(h = b\sin A\) and \(h = a\sin B\). Setting these equal gives \(b\sin A = a\sin B\), that is \(\dfrac{\sin A}{a} = \dfrac{\sin B}{b}\). The same trick with another height finishes the proof.

A quick SAA example: if \(A = 30°\), \(B = 45°\) and \(a = 10\), then \(b = \dfrac{a\sin B}{\sin A} = \dfrac{10 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{\frac12} = 10\sqrt2 \approx 14.14\), and \(C = 180° - 30° - 45° = 105°\).

The ambiguous case (SSA). When we know \(a\), \(b\) and angle \(A\), there may be no triangle, one triangle or two triangles. Picture it: side \(b\) is fixed at angle \(A\), and side \(a\) swings from \(C\) like a pendulum looking for the base line (Figure 6.8). The height is \(h = b\sin A\).

  • If \(a \lt h\): side \(a\) is too short to reach. No triangle.
  • If \(a = h\): it just touches. One right triangle.
  • If \(h \lt a \lt b\): it reaches the base in two places. Two triangles.
  • If \(a \ge b\): only one of the two places is on the correct side of \(A\). One triangle.

Algebraically, the Law of Sines gives \(\sin B\). If \(\sin B \gt 1\), there is no triangle. Otherwise there are two candidate angles, \(B_1 = \sin^{-1}(\sin B)\) and \(B_2 = 180° - B_1\) (both have the same sine), and each one is kept only if it leaves room for \(C\), that is, if \(A + B \lt 180°\).

Take note Your calculator's \(\sin^{-1}\) gives only the acute angle \(B_1\). In the SSA case, always test the obtuse angle \(B_2 = 180° - B_1\) too; forgetting it is the most common mistake in this section. Also, the Law of Sines cannot start a SAS or SSS problem, because we never know a side together with its opposite angle.

6.6The Law of Cosines

If we know two sides and the angle between them (SAS), or all three sides (SSS), the Law of Sines has nothing to start with. The Law of Cosines fills that gap. You can think of it as the Pythagorean Theorem with a correction term.

The Law of Cosines In any triangle \(ABC\),
\[ a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A \]
\[ b^2 = a^2 + c^2 - 2ac\cos B \]
\[ c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C \]
Use it for SAS (to find the third side) and for SSS (to find an angle). Solved for the angle: \(\cos C = \dfrac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}\).

When \(C = 90°\), \(\cos C = 0\) and we get back \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\). When \(C\) is obtuse, \(\cos C \lt 0\), so the correction adds to \(c^2\): the side across an obtuse angle is longer than the Pythagorean value. Here is a neat example: with \(a = 5\), \(b = 8\) and \(C = 60°\), \(c^2 = 25 + 64 - 2(5)(8)\left(\tfrac12\right) = 49\), so \(c = 7\).

Navigation and bearings. Ships and planes give directions as bearings. A bearing such as N 35° E means: face north, then turn \(35°\) toward the east. S 75° E means: face south, then turn \(75°\) toward the east. To use the Law of Cosines, we first find the angle between two paths from the bearings, usually with a quick sketch (Figure 6.9).

Heron's Formula. If we know the three sides of a triangle, we can find its area directly, without first finding an angle. Let \(s = \tfrac12(a + b + c)\) be the semiperimeter. Then the area is \(\sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}\). Check it on the 3-4-5 right triangle: \(s = 6\) and \(\sqrt{6 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = 6\), which equals \(\tfrac12 \cdot 3 \cdot 4\). It works.

Key equations
\[ c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C, \qquad \cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab} \]
\[ \text{Area} = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}, \qquad s = \frac{a + b + c}{2} \]
Take note In \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C\), compute \(2ab\cos C\) first and then subtract. Writing \((a^2 + b^2 - 2ab)\cos C\) is a common slip. In an SSS problem, find the largest angle first (opposite the longest side) with the Law of Cosines: \(\cos^{-1}\) correctly tells us if it is obtuse. The remaining angles are then acute, so the Law of Sines can finish safely.

★Key takeaways

  • 180° = π rad. Multiply by π/180 to get radians and by 180/π to get degrees. Arc length s = rθ, sector area A = ½r²θ and linear speed v = rω all need θ in radians.
  • SOH-CAH-TOA: sin θ = opp/hyp, cos θ = adj/hyp, tan θ = opp/adj; csc, sec and cot are their reciprocals (cot θ = adj/opp).
  • The 30°-60°-90° triangle (sides 1, √3, 2) and the 45°-45°-90° triangle (sides 1, 1, √2) give the exact values at 30°, 45° and 60° without a calculator.
  • For any angle, sin θ = y/r, cos θ = x/r, tan θ = y/x with r = √(x² + y²). Find the value at the reference angle, then attach the sign by quadrant (ASTC).
  • sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, tan²θ + 1 = sec²θ and 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ: one value plus the quadrant gives all six functions. The area of a triangle is ½ab sin C.
  • Inverse trig functions find the angle from a ratio; to simplify cos(sin⁻¹x), draw a right triangle with opp x and hyp 1.
  • Law of Sines for ASA, SAA and SSA (check for 0, 1 or 2 triangles); Law of Cosines for SAS and SSS; Heron's Formula gives the area from three sides.

Example 6.1 Degrees, radians and coterminal angles

(a) Convert \(225°\) to radians. (b) Convert \(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\) rad to degrees. (c) Convert 2 rad to degrees, to two decimal places. (d) Find the angle between \(0°\) and \(360°\) that is coterminal with \(1000°\).

(SRW, Angle Measure)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Angles \(225°\), \(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\) rad, 2 rad and \(1000°\).
  • Conversion: \(180° = \pi\) rad.
  • Asked: the conversions and a coterminal angle.
Step 1: Degrees to radians (multiply by π/180)

\[225° = 225 \cdot \frac{\pi}{180}\]

\[= \frac{225\pi}{180} = \frac{5\pi}{4}\ \text{rad}\]

Step 2: Radians to degrees (multiply by 180/π)

\[\frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6} \cdot \frac{180°}{\pi}\]

\[= 5 \cdot 30° = 150°\]

Take note that the \(\pi\) cancels. Without a \(\pi\), as in part (c), the answer is not a "nice" angle:

\[2\ \text{rad} = 2 \cdot \frac{180°}{\pi} = \frac{360°}{\pi} \approx 114.59°\]

Step 3: Coterminal angle (subtract full turns)

Let's subtract \(360°\) until we land between \(0°\) and \(360°\).

\[1000° - 360° = 640°\]

\[640° - 360° = 280°\]

Answer

(a) \(\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\) rad; (b) \(150°\); (c) about \(114.59°\); (d) \(280°\).

Check

\(\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\) is a bit more than \(\pi\), and \(225°\) is a bit more than \(180°\). Consistent. Also \(1000 = 2(360) + 280\).

Example 6.2 Eratosthenes measures the Earth

Around 240 BC, Eratosthenes noticed that at noon on the summer solstice the Sun was directly overhead at Syene, while at Alexandria, about 800 km due north, the Sun's rays made an angle of \(7.2°\) with the vertical. Assuming the Sun's rays are parallel, this means that the two cities subtend a central angle of \(7.2°\) at the centre of the Earth. Estimate the radius and the circumference of the Earth.

(SRW, Angle Measure)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Arc length (Syene to Alexandria): \(s = 800\) km.
  • Central angle: \(\theta = 7.2°\).
  • Asked: the radius \(r\) and the circumference \(2\pi r\).
Step 1: Convert the angle to radians

The formula \(s = r\theta\) needs radians.

\[\theta = 7.2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{25} \approx 0.12566\ \text{rad}\]

Step 2: Solve s = rθ for r

\[r = \frac{s}{\theta}\]

\[r = \frac{800}{\pi/25} = \frac{20\,000}{\pi}\]

\[r \approx 6366\ \text{km}\]

Step 3: The circumference

\[C = 2\pi r = 2\pi \cdot \frac{20\,000}{\pi} = 40\,000\ \text{km}\]

Another way to see it: \(7.2°\) is \(\tfrac{1}{50}\) of \(360°\), so the whole circle is \(50 \times 800 = 40\,000\) km.

Answer

The Earth's radius is about 6366 km, and its circumference about 40 000 km.

Check

The modern mean radius is about 6371 km, so this ancient estimate is remarkably close. If we had plugged in \(7.2\) instead of \(0.12566\), we would get \(r \approx 111\) km, which is clearly wrong.

Example 6.3 How fast is Manila moving?

Because the Earth spins once every 24 hours, Manila (latitude about \(14.6°\) N) moves around a circle of radius about 6165 km. (This radius is \(6371\cos 14.6°\), which you will understand after Section 6.2.) Find the angular speed of Manila in rad/h and its linear speed in km/h.

(SRW, Angle Measure)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • One full turn (\(2\pi\) rad) every \(t = 24\) h.
  • Radius of Manila's circle: \(r \approx 6165\) km.
  • Asked: \(\omega\) and \(v\).
Step 1: Angular speed

\[\omega = \frac{\theta}{t} = \frac{2\pi}{24}\]

\[\omega = \frac{\pi}{12} \approx 0.2618\ \text{rad/h}\]

Step 2: Linear speed v = rω

\[v = r\omega = 6165 \cdot \frac{\pi}{12}\]

\[v \approx 1614\ \text{km/h}\]

Answer

Manila turns at \(\dfrac{\pi}{12} \approx 0.262\) rad/h and moves at about 1614 km/h, faster than a passenger jet.

Check

Units: km \(\times\) rad/h = km/h (radians have no unit). Take note: every point on Earth has the same angular speed, but the linear speed depends on the radius. At the poles, \(r = 0\), so \(v = 0\).

Example 6.4 Six ratios and the special triangles

(a) A right triangle has legs 5 and 12. Find the six trigonometric ratios of the angle \(\theta\) opposite the leg 5. (b) Without a calculator, find the exact value of \(2\sin 30° + \sqrt3\tan 60° - \cos^2 45°\).

(SRW, Trigonometry of Right Triangles)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • opp \(= 5\), adj \(= 12\) (relative to \(\theta\)).
  • Special values from Figure 6.4.
  • Asked: six ratios; an exact value.
Step 1: Find the hypotenuse

\[\text{hyp} = \sqrt{5^2 + 12^2}\]

\[= \sqrt{169} = 13\]

Step 2: SOH-CAH-TOA, then the reciprocals

\[\sin\theta = \frac{5}{13}, \quad \cos\theta = \frac{12}{13}, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{5}{12}\]

\[\csc\theta = \frac{13}{5}, \quad \sec\theta = \frac{13}{12}, \quad \cot\theta = \frac{12}{5}\]

Take note: \(\cot\theta\) is adj/opp \(= \dfrac{12}{5}\), the reciprocal of \(\tan\theta\).

Step 3: Substitute the special values

From the special triangles, \(\sin 30° = \tfrac12\), \(\tan 60° = \sqrt3\), \(\cos 45° = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\).

\[2\left(\tfrac12\right) + \sqrt3 \cdot \sqrt3 - \left(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)^2\]

\[= 1 + 3 - \tfrac{2}{4}\]

\[= \tfrac{7}{2}\]

Answer

(a) \(\sin\theta = \tfrac{5}{13}\), \(\cos\theta = \tfrac{12}{13}\), \(\tan\theta = \tfrac{5}{12}\), \(\csc\theta = \tfrac{13}{5}\), \(\sec\theta = \tfrac{13}{12}\), \(\cot\theta = \tfrac{12}{5}\). (b) \(\tfrac72\).

Check

\(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = \tfrac{25}{169} + \tfrac{144}{169} = 1\). Good. For (b), a calculator in degree mode gives \(1 + 3 - 0.5 = 3.5\).

Example 6.5 Surveying a building in Manila

A surveyor stands 50 m from the base of an office building in Manila on level ground. The angle of elevation of the top of the building is \(62°\). (a) How tall is the building? (b) From the rooftop, what is the angle of depression of a car parked 120 m from the base of the building?

(SRW, Trigonometry of Right Triangles)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Distance to base (adj): 50 m; angle of elevation: \(62°\).
  • Car distance from base: 120 m.
  • Asked: the height \(h\); the angle of depression \(\alpha\).
Step 1: Choose the ratio

We know adj and want opp, so we use tangent (Figure 6.5).

\[\tan 62° = \frac{h}{50}\]

\[h = 50\tan 62°\]

\[h \approx 50(1.8807) \approx 94.04\ \text{m}\]

Step 2: Angle of depression

The angle of depression \(\alpha\) from the roof equals the angle of elevation of the roof as seen from the car (alternate angles). So, in the right triangle with opp \(= h\) and adj \(= 120\):

\[\tan\alpha = \frac{94.04}{120} \approx 0.7836\]

\[\alpha = \tan^{-1}(0.7836) \approx 38.08°\]

Answer

(a) The building is about 94.0 m tall. (b) The angle of depression is about \(38.1°\).

Check

The car is farther than the surveyor, so the line of sight is flatter: \(38.1° \lt 62°\), as expected. A building of 94 m is roughly 25 to 30 storeys, reasonable for Makati or Ortigas.

Example 6.6 Exact values of any angle

Find the exact values of (a) \(\sin 240°\), (b) \(\cos 135°\), (c) \(\tan(-60°)\), (d) \(\sec\dfrac{11\pi}{6}\).

(SRW, Trigonometric Functions of Angles)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Angles \(240°\), \(135°\), \(-60°\), \(\dfrac{11\pi}{6}\).
  • Recipe: reference angle, value, sign by quadrant (ASTC).
Step 1: sin 240°

\(240°\) is in Quadrant III, where sine is negative.

\[\bar\theta = 240° - 180° = 60°\]

\[\sin 240° = -\sin 60° = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\]

Step 2: cos 135°

\(135°\) is in Quadrant II, where cosine is negative.

\[\bar\theta = 180° - 135° = 45°\]

\[\cos 135° = -\cos 45° = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\]

Step 3: tan(−60°)

\(-60°\) is coterminal with \(300°\), in Quadrant IV, where tangent is negative.

\[\bar\theta = 360° - 300° = 60°\]

\[\tan(-60°) = -\tan 60° = -\sqrt3\]

Step 4: sec(11π/6)

\(\dfrac{11\pi}{6} = 330°\), in Quadrant IV, where cosine (and so secant) is positive.

\[\bar\theta = 360° - 330° = 30°\]

\[\sec 330° = \frac{1}{\cos 30°} = \frac{2}{\sqrt3} = \frac{2\sqrt3}{3}\]

Answer

(a) \(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\); (b) \(-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\); (c) \(-\sqrt3\); (d) \(\dfrac{2\sqrt3}{3}\).

Check

Calculator in degree mode: \(\sin 240° \approx -0.866\), \(\cos 135° \approx -0.707\), \(\tan(-60°) \approx -1.732\), \(1/\cos 330° \approx 1.155\). All match.

Example 6.7 From one value to all six

If \(\sin\theta = \dfrac35\) and \(\theta\) is in Quadrant II, find the other five trigonometric functions of \(\theta\).

(SRW, Trigonometric Functions of Angles)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\sin\theta = \tfrac35\).
  • \(\theta\) in Quadrant II: \(x \lt 0\), \(y \gt 0\).
  • Asked: \(\cos\theta\), \(\tan\theta\), \(\csc\theta\), \(\sec\theta\), \(\cot\theta\).
Step 1: Use the Pythagorean identity

\[\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta\]

\[= 1 - \frac{9}{25} = \frac{16}{25}\]

\[\cos\theta = \pm\frac45\]

Step 2: Choose the sign

In Quadrant II cosine is negative, so \(\cos\theta = -\dfrac45\).

Step 3: The rest from the quotient and reciprocal identities

\[\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{3/5}{-4/5} = -\frac34\]

\[\csc\theta = \frac53, \quad \sec\theta = -\frac54, \quad \cot\theta = -\frac43\]

Answer

\(\cos\theta = -\tfrac45\), \(\tan\theta = -\tfrac34\), \(\csc\theta = \tfrac53\), \(\sec\theta = -\tfrac54\), \(\cot\theta = -\tfrac43\).

Check

Using \(P(x, y) = (-4, 3)\) and \(r = 5\): \(\sin\theta = \tfrac35\), \(\cos\theta = -\tfrac45\). Also \(\tan^2\theta + 1 = \tfrac{9}{16} + 1 = \tfrac{25}{16} = \sec^2\theta\). Only sine and cosecant are positive, as ASTC says for Quadrant II.

Example 6.8 Inverse functions and compositions

(a) Find the exact value of \(\tan\left(\cos^{-1}\tfrac23\right)\). (b) Write \(\sec(\sin^{-1}x)\) as an algebraic expression in \(x\), for \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\). (c) A wheelchair ramp at RTU rises 0.75 m over a horizontal run of 9 m. Find its angle of inclination to two decimal places.

(SRW, Inverse Trigonometric Functions and Right Triangles)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\theta = \cos^{-1}\tfrac23\); \(\phi = \sin^{-1}x\).
  • Ramp: opp \(= 0.75\) m, adj \(= 9\) m.
Step 1: Draw the triangle for θ = cos⁻¹(2/3)

\(\cos\theta = \tfrac23\) and \(\theta\) is acute, so let adj \(= 2\), hyp \(= 3\).

\[\text{opp} = \sqrt{3^2 - 2^2} = \sqrt5\]

\[\tan\theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}} = \frac{\sqrt5}{2}\]

Step 2: Draw the triangle for φ = sin⁻¹x

As in Figure 6.7, opp \(= x\), hyp \(= 1\), adj \(= \sqrt{1 - x^2}\).

\[\sec\phi = \frac{\text{hyp}}{\text{adj}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}\]

Step 3: The ramp angle

\[\tan\theta = \frac{0.75}{9} \approx 0.08333\]

\[\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.08333) \approx 4.76°\]

Answer

(a) \(\dfrac{\sqrt5}{2}\); (b) \(\sec(\sin^{-1}x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}\); (c) about \(4.76°\).

Check

(a) \(\cos^{-1}(2/3) \approx 48.19°\) and \(\tan 48.19° \approx 1.118 \approx \sqrt5/2\). (b) At \(x = 0.6\): \(\sin^{-1}0.6 \approx 36.87°\), \(\sec 36.87° = 1.25 = 1/\sqrt{0.64}\). (c) A ratio of 1:12 is the usual accessibility guideline, about \(4.76°\).

Example 6.9 The ambiguous case

Solve triangle \(ABC\) if \(A = 40°\), \(a = 8\) and \(b = 11\). Round to two decimal places.

(SRW, The Law of Sines)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • SSA: \(A = 40°\), \(a = 8\), \(b = 11\).

\[\frac{\sin B}{11} = \frac{\sin 40°}{8}\]

\[B_1 = \sin^{-1}(0.8838) \approx 62.11°\]

\[C_1 = 180° - 40° - 62.11° = 77.89°\]

Step 4: Triangle 2

\[C_2 = 180° - 40° - 117.89° = 22.11°\]

\[c_2 = \frac{8\sin 22.11°}{\sin 40°} \approx 4.68\]

Triangle 1: \(B \approx 62.11°\), \(C \approx 77.89°\), \(c \approx 12.17\). Triangle 2: \(B \approx 117.89°\), \(C \approx 22.11°\), \(c \approx 4.68\).

The height test agrees: \(h = 11\sin 40° \approx 7.07\) and \(7.07 \lt 8 \lt 11\), so two triangles. In each triangle the largest side is opposite the largest angle (\(c_1\) with \(C_1\); \(b\) with \(B_2\)).

Example 6.10 Two weather stations sight a waterspout

Two coastal weather stations \(A\) and \(B\) are 40 km apart along a straight shoreline. Both spot a waterspout \(W\) over the sea. The angle between the shoreline and the line of sight is \(48°\) at \(A\) and \(75°\) at \(B\). How far is the waterspout from each station? How far is it from the shore?

(SRW, The Law of Sines)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • ASA: \(AB = 40\) km, \(\angle A = 48°\), \(\angle B = 75°\).
  • Asked: \(AW\), \(BW\) and the perpendicular distance to the shore.
Step 1: The third angle

\[\angle W = 180° - 48° - 75° = 57°\]

Now we know side \(AB\) and its opposite angle \(W\), so the Law of Sines applies.

Step 2: Distance from A (opposite angle B)

\[\frac{AW}{\sin 75°} = \frac{40}{\sin 57°}\]

\[AW = \frac{40\sin 75°}{\sin 57°} \approx 46.07\ \text{km}\]

Step 3: Distance from B (opposite angle A)

\[BW = \frac{40\sin 48°}{\sin 57°} \approx 35.44\ \text{km}\]

Step 4: Distance from the shore

Drop a perpendicular from \(W\) to the shoreline. In the right triangle at \(A\):

\[d = AW\sin 48° \approx 46.07(0.7431) \approx 34.24\ \text{km}\]

Answer

The waterspout is about 46.07 km from \(A\), 35.44 km from \(B\), and 34.24 km from the shore.

Check

Using \(B\) instead: \(BW\sin 75° \approx 35.44(0.9659) \approx 34.24\) km. Same distance. Also the shortest side \(BW\) is opposite the smallest angle \(48°\).

Example 6.11 Two ships and their bearings

Two ships leave a port in Batangas at the same time. One sails on a bearing of N 35° E at 20 km/h; the other sails on a bearing of S 75° E at 15 km/h. How far apart are they after 2 hours?

(SRW, The Law of Cosines)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Ship 1: N 35° E, \(20 \times 2 = 40\) km.
  • Ship 2: S 75° E, \(15 \times 2 = 30\) km.
  • Asked: the distance \(c\) between them.
Step 1: The angle between the paths

From Figure 6.9, ship 1 is \(35°\) east of north and ship 2 is \(75°\) east of south. North and south make a straight angle, so

\[C = 180° - 35° - 75° = 70°\]

Step 2: Law of Cosines (SAS)

\[c^2 = 40^2 + 30^2 - 2(40)(30)\cos 70°\]

\[c^2 = 1600 + 900 - 2400(0.34202)\]

\[c^2 \approx 2500 - 820.85 = 1679.15\]

\[c \approx 40.98\ \text{km}\]

Answer

After 2 hours the ships are about 40.98 km apart.

Check

If the angle were \(90°\), the distance would be \(\sqrt{2500} = 50\) km. Since \(70° \lt 90°\), the ships are closer than that: \(40.98 \lt 50\). Makes sense.

Example 6.12 A triangular lot: angles and area

A triangular lot in Quezon City has sides 25 m, 32 m and 41 m. (a) Find its largest angle. (b) Find its area with Heron's Formula. (c) If land there sells for ₱25,000 per square metre, about how much is the lot worth?

(SRW, The Law of Cosines)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • SSS: \(a = 25\), \(b = 32\), \(c = 41\) (metres).
  • Price: ₱25,000 per m².
  • Asked: the largest angle, the area and the value.
Step 1: Largest angle (opposite the longest side, c = 41)

\[\cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}\]

\[\cos C = \frac{625 + 1024 - 1681}{2(25)(32)}\]

\[\cos C = \frac{-32}{1600} = -0.02\]

\[C = \cos^{-1}(-0.02) \approx 91.15°\]

The angle is slightly obtuse. Take note that \(\cos^{-1}\) of a negative number gives an obtuse angle automatically.

Step 2: Semiperimeter

\[s = \frac{25 + 32 + 41}{2} = 49\]

Step 3: Heron's Formula

\[\text{Area} = \sqrt{49(49 - 25)(49 - 32)(49 - 41)}\]

\[= \sqrt{49 \cdot 24 \cdot 17 \cdot 8}\]

\[= \sqrt{159\,936} \approx 399.92\ \text{m}^2\]

Step 4: Value of the lot

\[399.92 \times 25\,000 \approx 9\,998\,000\]

Answer

(a) The largest angle is about \(91.15°\). (b) The area is about 399.92 m². (c) The lot is worth about ₱10.0 million.

Check

Using \(\tfrac12 ab\sin C = \tfrac12(25)(32)\sin 91.15° \approx 400(0.9998) \approx 399.92\) m². Both area formulas agree.
