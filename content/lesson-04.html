4.1Exponential Functions

So far, the variable in our functions has always been in the base: \(x^2\), \(x^3\), \(\sqrt{x}\). In this module we put the variable up in the exponent. This small change gives us the functions that describe bacteria doubling in a petri dish, money growing in a bank, and carbon-14 slowly disappearing from an old bone. Let's start with the definition.

Definition An exponential function has the form
\[ (1)\quad f(x) = a\,b^x, \qquad b \gt 0,\; b \ne 1 \]
where \(a\) is the initial value (since \(f(0) = a\)), \(b\) is the base, and \(x\) is the exponent. If \(b \gt 1\), we have exponential growth; if \(0 \lt b \lt 1\), we have exponential decay.

Why do we need \(b \gt 0\)? If \(b = -4\), then \(b^{1/2} = \sqrt{-4}\) is not a real number, so the function would have holes everywhere. And \(b = 1\) is excluded because \(1^x = 1\) is just a constant.

A quick table. Let's compare \(f(x) = 2^x\) (here \(a = 1\), \(b = 2\)) with \(g(x) = 3(0.5)^x\) (here \(a = 3\), \(b = 0.5\)).

\(x\)\(-2\)\(-1\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)\(3\)
\(2^x\)\(\tfrac14\)\(\tfrac12\)1248
\(3(0.5)^x\)12631.50.750.375

Notice the pattern. Each time \(x\) goes up by 1, \(2^x\) is multiplied by 2, while \(3(0.5)^x\) is multiplied by 0.5 (it is halved). A linear function adds the same amount at each step; an exponential function multiplies by the same factor. From this, we know that \(2^x\) is increasing (growth) and \(3(0.5)^x\) is decreasing (decay). See Figure 4.1.

Key features of \(y = b^x\). The domain is all real numbers, \((-\infty, \infty)\), because we can raise a positive base to any power. The range is only \((0, \infty)\): a power of a positive number is never zero and never negative. The graph passes through \((0, 1)\) because \(b^0 = 1\), and the \(x\)-axis (\(y = 0\)) is a horizontal asymptote. For growth the graph hugs the asymptote on the left; for decay it hugs it on the right. Also, \(\left(\tfrac12\right)^x = 2^{-x}\), so the graph of \((1/2)^x\) is the reflection of \(2^x\) in the \(y\)-axis.

Transformations. Everything we learned about shifting and reflecting graphs applies here. Take \(f(x) = 2^x + 1\). This is still exponential growth, but the whole graph moves up 1 unit, so the horizontal asymptote moves to \(y = 1\) and the range becomes \((1, \infty)\). Similarly, \(2^{x-2}\) is \(2^x\) shifted right 2 units, and \(-2^x\) is \(2^x\) reflected in the \(x\)-axis (range \((-\infty, 0)\)). Figure 4.2 shows all three.

Take note \(-2^x\) is not \((-2)^x\). In \(-2^x\) the exponent acts on 2 only, and then we take the negative; \((-2)^x\) is not even an exponential function, since its base is negative. Also, do not mix up \(x^2\) (a power function: variable base) with \(2^x\) (an exponential function: variable exponent). At \(x = 10\), \(x^2 = 100\) but \(2^x = 1024\); in the long run every exponential growth beats every power.

4.2The Natural Exponential Function

Any positive number other than 1 can be a base, but one base is so useful that it gets its own name. It comes from a very practical question about money: what happens if a bank compounds interest more and more often?

Compound interest. If a principal \(P\) earns an annual rate \(r\), compounded \(n\) times a year, then after \(t\) years the amount is equation (10) below. For example, if we deposit 2000 pesos at 5% a year compounded quarterly (\(n = 4\)), after 2 years we have \(A = 2000\left(1 + \tfrac{0.05}{4}\right)^{4(2)} = 2000(1.0125)^8 \approx 2208.97\) pesos.

Now suppose we invest just 1 peso at a (very generous) 100% rate for 1 year. Then \(A = \left(1 + \tfrac1n\right)^n\). Let's see what happens as \(n\) grows.

\(n\)1 (yearly)4 (quarterly)12 (monthly)365 (daily)8760 (hourly)100 000
\(\left(1 + \tfrac1n\right)^n\)22.441412.613042.714572.718132.71827

The amount does not grow without bound. It levels off near 2.71828 (Figure 4.4). That limiting value is the number \(e\), Euler's number. Like \(\pi\), it is irrational: \(e \approx 2.718281828459\ldots\)

Definition and key equations The natural exponential function is the exponential function with base \(e\):
\[ (2)\quad f(x) = e^x, \qquad e = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1 + \frac1n\right)^n \approx 2.71828 \]
\[ (10)\quad A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt} \quad \text{(compounded } n \text{ times a year)} \]
\[ (10')\quad A = Pe^{rt} \quad \text{(compounded continuously)} \]

Since \(2 \lt e \lt 3\), the graph of \(e^x\) lies between the graphs of \(2^x\) and \(3^x\) (Figure 4.3). It has every feature of \(b^x\) with \(b \gt 1\): domain \((-\infty, \infty)\), range \((0, \infty)\), \(y\)-intercept 1, and horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\). Its mirror image \(e^{-x} = (1/e)^x\) is a decay function. For instance, \(e^0 = 1\), \(e^1 \approx 2.71828\), \(e^2 \approx 7.38906\), while \(e^{-1} \approx 0.36788\) and \(e^{-2} \approx 0.13534\). A constant inside the exponent changes the speed: \(5e^{2x}\) starts at 5 and grows twice as fast, \(5e^{2} \approx 36.945\), \(5e^{4} \approx 272.991\).

Why is \(e\) "natural"? If the interest is compounded continuously (every instant), we let \(n \to \infty\) in (10), and the amount becomes \(A = Pe^{rt}\). This is the same function that models any quantity that changes continuously in proportion to its size: populations, radioactive atoms, the charge in a capacitor. Let's compare: 2000 pesos at 5% for 2 years compounded continuously gives \(A = 2000e^{0.05(2)} = 2000e^{0.1} \approx 2210.34\) pesos, only about 1.37 pesos more than compounding quarterly.

Take note In \(A = P\left(1 + \tfrac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\), the rate \(r\) is a decimal (5% is 0.05, not 5), and the exponent is the total number of periods \(nt\), not \(t\). Also, \(e\) is a number, not a variable: \(e^2\) is about 7.389, and on a calculator we use the \(e^x\) key, not \(2.7^2\).

4.3Logarithmic Functions

Every exponential function \(f(x) = b^x\) passes the Horizontal Line Test: it is always increasing or always decreasing. So it is one-to-one and it has an inverse function. This inverse is the logarithmic function. In words, a logarithm answers the question: "To what exponent must we raise \(b\) to get \(x\)?"

Definition Let \(b \gt 0\), \(b \ne 1\). If \(y = b^x\), then
\[ (3)\quad x = \log_b y \]
So \(\log_b x = y\) means exactly the same as \(b^y = x\). The logarithm is an exponent. The common logarithm has base 10 and is written \(\log x\) (no base shown); the natural logarithm has base \(e\) and is written \(\ln x = \log_e x\).

Switching forms. Let's practise. Take note of where each number goes: the base stays the base, the logarithm becomes the exponent.

Logarithmic formExponential formIn words
\(\log_3 9 = 2\)\(3^2 = 9\)3 squared is 9
\(\log 100 = 2\)\(10^2 = 100\)no base written means base 10
\(\log_5 625 = 4\)\(5^4 = 625\)5 to the fourth is 625
\(\ln t = 2\)\(t = e^2\)base \(e\)
\(\log_2 \tfrac18 = -3\)\(2^{-3} = \tfrac18\)a log can be negative

Properties. Directly from the definition we get four facts. \(\log_b 1 = 0\) because \(b^0 = 1\). \(\log_b b = 1\) because \(b^1 = b\). And since \(\log_b\) and \(b^x\) undo each other, \(\log_b b^x = x\) and \(b^{\log_b x} = x\). For example, \(\ln e^7 = 7\), \(10^{\log 3} = 3\), and \(\log_4 64 = \log_4 4^3 = 3\).

Key equations
\[ \log_b x = y \iff b^y = x \]
\[ \log_b 1 = 0, \qquad \log_b b = 1 \]
\[ \log_b b^x = x, \qquad b^{\log_b x} = x \quad (x \gt 0) \]

Graphs. The graph of an inverse function is the reflection of the original graph in the line \(y = x\). So we get the graph of \(y = \log_2 x\) by swapping the coordinates of points on \(y = 2^x\): \((0, 1) \to (1, 0)\), \((1, 2) \to (2, 1)\), \((3, 8) \to (8, 3)\) (Figure 4.5). Domain and range swap too. The domain of \(\log_b x\) is \((0, \infty)\), its range is \((-\infty, \infty)\), its \(x\)-intercept is 1, and the \(y\)-axis (\(x = 0\)) is a vertical asymptote. For \(b \gt 1\), the graph rises, but very slowly: \(\log x\) only reaches 6 at \(x = 1\,000\,000\).

Transformations and domains. Since we can only take the logarithm of a positive number, the domain of a transformed logarithm comes from "inside \(\gt 0\)". For \(g(x) = \log_2(x - 3) + 1\), we need \(x - 3 \gt 0\), so the domain is \((3, \infty)\) and the vertical asymptote is \(x = 3\). The graph is \(\log_2 x\) shifted right 3 and up 1. Its \(x\)-intercept: \(\log_2(x - 3) = -1\), so \(x - 3 = 2^{-1}\), \(x = 3.5\). Figure 4.6 compares it with \(\log_2 x\), \(\ln x\) and \(\log x\); notice that all three basic logarithms pass through \((1, 0)\).

Take note \(\log_b x\) is defined only for \(x \gt 0\). There is no real number \(y\) with \(10^y = -5\) or \(10^y = 0\), so \(\log(-5)\) and \(\log 0\) do not exist. A logarithm itself, however, can be negative: \(\log 0.01 = -2\). Do not confuse "the input must be positive" with "the output must be positive".

4.4Laws of Logarithms

A logarithm is an exponent, so the laws of exponents turn directly into laws of logarithms. For example, \(b^m b^n = b^{m+n}\) says "multiplying the numbers adds the exponents", and that becomes the Product Rule: the log of a product is the sum of the logs. This is exactly why, before calculators, astronomers like Kepler used log tables: they turned long multiplications into additions.

Table 1. Laws of Logarithms For \(b \gt 0\), \(b \ne 1\), \(M \gt 0\), \(N \gt 0\) and any real \(k\):
\[ (4)\quad \log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b N \quad \text{(Product Rule)} \]
\[ (5)\quad \log_b\!\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b M - \log_b N \quad \text{(Quotient Rule)} \]
\[ (6)\quad \log_b\left(M^k\right) = k\log_b M \quad \text{(Power Rule)} \]
\[ (7)\quad \log_b a = \frac{\log_c a}{\log_c b} \quad \text{(Change of Base; usually } c = 10 \text{ or } e) \]

Let's see each law at work with numbers we can check in our heads:

  • Product Rule: \(\log_2(8 \cdot 4) = \log_2 8 + \log_2 4 = 3 + 2 = 5\). Check: \(\log_2 32 = 5\).
  • Quotient Rule: \(\log\left(\tfrac{1000}{10}\right) = \log 1000 - \log 10 = 3 - 1 = 2\). Check: \(\log 100 = 2\).
  • Power Rule: \(\log_3 9^2 = 2\log_3 9 = 2(2) = 4\). Check: \(\log_3 81 = 4\).
  • Change of Base: \(\log_2 7 = \dfrac{\log 7}{\log 2} = \dfrac{0.84510}{0.30103} \approx 2.807\). Check: \(2^{2.807} \approx 7\).

Expanding and combining. We use the laws in both directions. To expand, we break one logarithm into many: \(\log_b\left(\dfrac{x^3}{y\sqrt{z}}\right) = 3\log_b x - \log_b y - \tfrac12\log_b z\). To combine (condense), we go backwards: first move coefficients up as powers, then join sums into products and differences into quotients. So \(\log x + 2\log y - \log z = \log x + \log y^2 - \log z = \log\dfrac{xy^2}{z}\).

Why Change of Base? Calculators only have \(\log\) and \(\ln\) keys. To find \(\log_5 20\), we let \(y = \log_5 20\), so \(5^y = 20\). Taking \(\log\) of both sides, \(y\log 5 = \log 20\), and \(y = \dfrac{\log 20}{\log 5} \approx 1.861\). That short argument is the proof of formula (7). Using \(\ln\) instead gives the same answer: \(\dfrac{\ln 20}{\ln 5} = \dfrac{2.9957}{1.6094} \approx 1.861\).

Take note There is no rule for the log of a sum: \(\log(M + N) \ne \log M + \log N\). Also \(\dfrac{\log M}{\log N} \ne \log\dfrac{M}{N}\), and \((\log M)^k \ne k\log M\). For instance, \(\log(10 + 10) = \log 20 \approx 1.301\), but \(\log 10 + \log 10 = 2\). When in doubt, test the "rule" with simple numbers.

4.5Exponential and Logarithmic Equations

Now we use everything so far to solve equations where the unknown sits in an exponent or inside a logarithm. Since \(b^x\) and \(\log_b x\) are inverses, each kind of equation is solved by applying the other function.

Guidelines Exponential equations. (1) Isolate the exponential term. (2) If both sides can be written with the same base, equate the exponents; otherwise take \(\log\) or \(\ln\) of both sides and use the Power Rule to "bring down the exponent". (3) Solve for the variable.
Logarithmic equations. (1) Use the Laws of Logarithms to combine the logs into a single logarithm, and isolate it. (2) Rewrite in exponential form. (3) Solve, and check every answer in the original equation.
\[ b^u = b^v \;\Rightarrow\; u = v, \qquad b^x = c \;\Rightarrow\; x = \log_b c = \frac{\ln c}{\ln b} \]
\[ \log_b u = c \;\Rightarrow\; u = b^c \]

Same base. To solve \(2^x = 8\), write \(8 = 2^3\); then \(2^x = 2^3\) and \(x = 3\). For \(3^{2x-1} = 81 = 3^4\), the exponents must be equal: \(2x - 1 = 4\), so \(x = \tfrac52\).

Different bases. We cannot write 20 as a nice power of 5, so for \(5^x = 20\) we take logarithms: \(x = \log_5 20 = \dfrac{\log 20}{\log 5} \approx 1.861\). This means we must raise 5 to about 1.861 to get 20. Graphically, it is the \(x\)-coordinate where the curve \(y = 5^x\) meets the line \(y = 20\) (Figure 4.7). Desmos is very good at this: type both sides as two functions and click the intersection.

Logarithmic equations. For \(\log_2 x = 3\), rewrite in exponential form: \(x = 2^3 = 8\). For \(\ln(x + 2) = 3\), we get \(x + 2 = e^3\), so \(x = e^3 - 2 \approx 18.09\). When there are two logs, we combine them first. Let's solve \(\log_3(x - 1) + \log_3(x + 1) = 2\). By the Product Rule, \(\log_3[(x - 1)(x + 1)] = 2\), so \((x - 1)(x + 1) = 3^2\), \(x^2 - 1 = 9\), and \(x^2 = 10\). That gives \(x = \pm\sqrt{10}\). But the original logs need \(x - 1 \gt 0\), that is, \(x \gt 1\). Since \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16 \gt 1\) and \(-\sqrt{10} \approx -3.16 \lt 1\), the only solution is \(x = \sqrt{10}\).

Equations of quadratic type. Sometimes a substitution helps. In \(e^{2x} - e^x - 6 = 0\), notice that \(e^{2x} = (e^x)^2\). Let \(w = e^x\): \(w^2 - w - 6 = 0\), \((w - 3)(w + 2) = 0\). So \(e^x = 3\) or \(e^x = -2\). The second is impossible (\(e^x \gt 0\) always), so \(x = \ln 3 \approx 1.099\).

Take note Combining logs can create extraneous solutions: \((x - 1)(x + 1)\) is positive at \(x = -\sqrt{10}\), even though \(x - 1\) and \(x + 1\) are both negative there. Always substitute back into the original equation. Also, \(\log_3(x - 1) + \log_3(x + 1) = 2\) does not become \((x - 1) + (x + 1) = 9\); we may only "drop the logs" after combining them into a single logarithm.

4.6Modeling with Exponential Functions

Exponential functions describe any quantity that changes by the same percentage in equal time steps: populations, money, drugs leaving the bloodstream, radioactive atoms. Let's go back to our general model \(y = ab^x\), where \(a\) is the initial value and \(b\) is the growth or decay factor.

Key equations With initial amount \(a\), rate \(r\) per period and \(t\) periods:
\[ (8)\quad y = a(1 + r)^t \quad \text{(growth at rate } r) \]
\[ (9)\quad y = a(1 - r)^t \quad \text{(decay at rate } r) \]
\[ n(t) = n_0 e^{rt} \quad \text{(exponential growth, relative rate } r) \]
\[ m(t) = m_0 e^{-rt}, \quad r = \frac{\ln 2}{h} \quad \text{(radioactive decay, half-life } h) \]
\[ n(t) = \frac{M}{1 + Ae^{-rt}}, \quad A = \frac{M - n_0}{n_0} \quad \text{(logistic growth, carrying capacity } M) \]

Doubling. Suppose a bacteria culture starts with 200 cells and doubles every 3 hours. After 3 hours there are \(200 \cdot 2\), after 6 hours \(200 \cdot 2^2\), and after \(t\) hours \(200 \cdot 2^{t/3}\) (the exponent counts how many 3-hour periods have passed). So \(P(t) = 200 \cdot 2^{t/3}\), and after 9 hours \(P(9) = 200 \cdot 2^3 = 1600\) cells (Figure 4.8, left). In general, a quantity with doubling time \(d\) is \(n(t) = n_0 \cdot 2^{t/d}\).

Decay at a fixed rate. If carbon-14 decays by about 11.5% every 1000 years, then each 1000 years 88.5% remains, and \(A(t) = A_0(0.885)^t\) with \(t\) in thousands of years. After 2000 years, \(A = A_0(0.885)^2 \approx 0.783A_0\): about 78.3% remains.

Half-life. Physicists describe radioactive decay by the half-life \(h\), the time for half of any sample to decay. Since \(m(h) = \tfrac12 m_0\), we get \(m_0e^{-rh} = \tfrac12 m_0\), so \(-rh = \ln\tfrac12 = -\ln 2\), and \(r = \dfrac{\ln 2}{h}\). For carbon-14, \(h = 5730\) years, so \(r = \dfrac{\ln 2}{5730} \approx 0.000121\) per year. Every 5730 years the amount is halved: 50%, 25%, 12.5%, ... (Figure 4.8, right). Archaeologists use this to date bones, wood and charcoal.

Logistic growth. Real populations cannot grow forever; food and space run out. The logistic model starts out almost exponential but levels off at the carrying capacity \(M\). For example, a fishpond in Laguna stocked with 500 tilapia that can support at most 5000 fish, with \(r = 0.4\) per year, has \(A = \dfrac{5000 - 500}{500} = 9\) and \(n(t) = \dfrac{5000}{1 + 9e^{-0.4t}}\). After 5 years, \(n(5) = \dfrac{5000}{1 + 9e^{-2}} \approx 2254\) fish, and as \(t \to \infty\), \(e^{-0.4t} \to 0\), so \(n(t) \to 5000\).

Take note Match the exponent to the time unit. If a culture doubles every 3 hours, the exponent is \(t/3\), not \(3t\) and not \(t\). And a decay rate of 11.5% gives the factor \(1 - 0.115 = 0.885\), not \(0.115\). Before you trust a model, check it at \(t = 0\) (you should get the initial value) and at one doubling time or half-life.

4.7Logarithmic Scales

A logarithmic scale is a nonlinear scale for quantities that span a huge range of values. On an ordinary (linear) scale, each step adds a fixed amount. On a logarithmic scale, each step multiplies by a fixed factor: equal distances represent equal ratios, not equal differences.

Why do we use them? The quietest sound we can hear and a jet engine differ in intensity by a factor of about \(10^{14}\). The Sun looks about \(3 \times 10^{11}\) times brighter in our sky than Polaris. Writing such numbers on a linear scale is hopeless, but their logarithms are small, friendly numbers. Logarithmic scales also match how our senses work: our ears and eyes respond to ratios, not differences.

Key equations A general logarithmic scale compares a quantity \(Q\) with a reference \(Q_0\):
\[ (11)\quad S = k\log_b\left(\frac{Q}{Q_0}\right) \]
where \(b\) is the base (usually 10) and \(k\) scales the result into convenient units.
ScaleUsed forFormula
DecibelSound intensity level\(L = 10\log\dfrac{I}{I_0}\), \(I_0 = 10^{-12}\) W/m²(12)
Richter scaleEarthquake magnitude\(M = \log\dfrac{A}{A_0}\)(13)
pH scaleAcidity of a solution\(\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}^+]\)(14)
Astronomical magnitudeBrightness of stars\(m_2 - m_1 = -2.5\log\dfrac{I_2}{I_1}\)(15)

In all of these, what matters is the ratio between two quantities. Let's see three quick cases. A sound with intensity \(10^{-6}\) W/m² has level \(L = 10\log\dfrac{10^{-6}}{10^{-12}} = 10\log 10^6 = 60\) dB, about a normal conversation. A solution with \([\text{H}^+] = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}\) M has \(\text{pH} = -\log 10^{-4} = 4\), acidic; each drop of 1 in pH means 10 times more acidic. If one earthquake's waves are 100 times larger than another's, the difference in magnitude is \(\log 100 = 2\): a magnitude 7 quake has waves 100 times larger than a magnitude 5.

Stellar magnitudes. About 2000 years ago Hipparchus sorted the stars he could see into six classes: the brightest were "first magnitude", the faintest "sixth magnitude". In 1856, Norman Pogson made this precise: a difference of 5 magnitudes is exactly a brightness ratio of 100. So one magnitude is a ratio of \(100^{1/5} \approx 2.512\), and that is where the \(-2.5\) in formula (15) comes from. Take note of the minus sign: brighter objects have smaller magnitudes, and the very brightest have negative ones (Figure 4.9). If star A is 100 times brighter than star B, then \(m_A - m_B = -2.5\log 100 = -5\): its magnitude is 5 units lower.

Solving (15) for the ratio gives the form we use most: \(\dfrac{I_2}{I_1} = 10^{-0.4(m_2 - m_1)}\). Astronomers also compare apparent magnitude \(m\) with absolute magnitude \(M\) (how bright the star would look from 10 parsecs) through the distance modulus \(m - M = 5\log\dfrac{d}{10\text{ pc}}\), which turns brightness into distance.

Take note A logarithmic scale hides huge differences behind small numbers. A magnitude 8 earthquake is not "a bit more" than a magnitude 6; its waves are \(10^2 = 100\) times larger. Likewise, 80 dB is not twice as intense as 40 dB, it is \(10^4 = 10\,000\) times as intense. Always convert the difference on the scale back into a ratio.

★Key takeaways

  • An exponential function f(x) = a·bˣ (b > 0, b ≠ 1) multiplies by the same factor b at each step: growth if b > 1, decay if 0 < b < 1. Its domain is all reals, its range (0, ∞), and y = 0 is its horizontal asymptote (before shifting).
  • e ≈ 2.71828 is the limit of (1 + 1/n)ⁿ. Compound interest is A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt); continuous compounding is A = Pe^(rt).
  • log_b x = y means b^y = x: a logarithm is an exponent. y = log_b x is the inverse of y = bˣ, with domain (0, ∞), range all reals, x-intercept 1 and vertical asymptote x = 0.
  • Laws of Logarithms: log(MN) = log M + log N, log(M/N) = log M − log N, log Mᵏ = k log M, and log_b a = ln a / ln b. There is no rule for log(M + N).
  • To solve bˣ = c, take logs: x = ln c / ln b. To solve a log equation, combine into one log, rewrite in exponential form, and check for extraneous solutions.
  • Growth with doubling time d: n₀·2^(t/d); continuous growth n₀e^(rt); radioactive decay m₀e^(−rt) with r = ln 2 / h; logistic growth levels off at the carrying capacity M.
  • Logarithmic scales (decibels, Richter, pH, stellar magnitudes) turn ratios into differences. Five magnitudes = a brightness ratio of 100, and smaller magnitudes are brighter.

Example 4.1 Graphing a transformed exponential function

Let \(g(x) = 2^{x-1} + 3\). Describe how to get its graph from \(y = 2^x\), and find its domain, range, horizontal asymptote and \(y\)-intercept. Does it have an \(x\)-intercept?

(SRW, Exponential Functions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Basic graph \(y = 2^x\): domain \((-\infty, \infty)\), range \((0, \infty)\), asymptote \(y = 0\), passes through \((0, 1)\).
  • New function: \(g(x) = 2^{x-1} + 3\).
  • Asked: the transformations, domain, range, asymptote and intercepts.
Step 1: Read the transformations

Replacing \(x\) by \(x - 1\) shifts the graph right 1 unit. Adding 3 shifts it up 3 units.

\[y = 2^x \;\to\; y = 2^{x-1} \;\to\; y = 2^{x-1} + 3\]

Step 2: Domain, range and asymptote

Shifting right does not change the domain. Shifting up 3 lifts the asymptote from \(y = 0\) to \(y = 3\), and the range from \((0, \infty)\) to \((3, \infty)\).

\[\text{Domain: } (-\infty, \infty), \qquad \text{Range: } (3, \infty), \qquad \text{H.A.: } y = 3\]

Step 3: The y-intercept

Let's substitute \(x = 0\).

\[g(0) = 2^{0-1} + 3\]

\[= \tfrac12 + 3\]

\[= 3.5\]

Step 4: The x-intercept

An \(x\)-intercept needs \(g(x) = 0\), that is, \(2^{x-1} = -3\). But a power of 2 is always positive. Take note: the range \((3, \infty)\) does not even contain 0.

Answer

Shift \(y = 2^x\) right 1 and up 3. Domain \((-\infty, \infty)\), range \((3, \infty)\), horizontal asymptote \(y = 3\), \(y\)-intercept \((0, 3.5)\), and no \(x\)-intercept. The function is increasing (exponential growth).

Check

The point \((0, 1)\) on \(2^x\) moves to \((1, 4)\): indeed \(g(1) = 2^0 + 3 = 4\). And \(g(-10) = 2^{-11} + 3 \approx 3.0005\), just above the asymptote.

Example 4.2 Exponential and natural exponential values

Make a table of values at \(x = 0, 1, 2\) for \(f(x) = 3(0.5)^x\), \(g(x) = e^{-x}\) and \(h(x) = 5e^{2x}\). Decide which are growth and which are decay, and which grows fastest.

(SRW, The Natural Exponential Function)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(f(x) = 3(0.5)^x\): \(a = 3\), \(b = 0.5\).
  • \(g(x) = e^{-x} = (1/e)^x\): \(a = 1\), \(b = 1/e \approx 0.368\).
  • \(h(x) = 5e^{2x} = 5(e^2)^x\): \(a = 5\), \(b = e^2 \approx 7.389\).
Step 1: Values of f

\[f(0) = 3(0.5)^0 = 3\]

\[f(1) = 3(0.5) = 1.5\]

\[f(2) = 3(0.25) = 0.75\]

Step 2: Values of g

\[g(0) = e^0 = 1\]

\[g(1) = \frac1e \approx 0.36788\]

\[g(2) = \frac{1}{e^2} \approx 0.13534\]

Step 3: Values of h

\[h(0) = 5e^0 = 5\]

\[h(1) = 5e^2 \approx 36.945\]

\[h(2) = 5e^4 \approx 272.991\]

Step 4: Growth or decay?

Let's look at the bases. \(f\) and \(g\) have bases less than 1, so they decay; \(h\) has base \(e^2 \gt 1\), so it grows. From this, we know that \(h\) is multiplied by about 7.389 for every unit step in \(x\).

Answer

\(f\): 3, 1.5, 0.75 (decay, halves each step). \(g\): 1, 0.368, 0.135 (decay, factor \(1/e\)). \(h\): 5, 36.945, 272.991 (growth, factor \(e^2\)); \(h\) grows fastest, because of the multiplier 2 in the exponent.

Check

Ratios: \(0.75/1.5 = 0.5\), \(0.13534/0.36788 \approx 0.368 = 1/e\), \(272.991/36.945 \approx 7.389 = e^2\). Constant ratios confirm each function is exponential.

Example 4.4 Exponential form, logarithmic form, and evaluating logs

(a) Write \(\log_5 625 = 4\) and \(\ln t = 2\) in exponential form, and \(2^{-5} = \tfrac{1}{32}\) in logarithmic form. (b) Evaluate \(\log_2 \tfrac{1}{32}\), \(\log_{9} 3\), \(\log 0.001\) and \(\ln e^7\) without a calculator.

(SRW, Logarithmic Functions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\log_b x = y \iff b^y = x\).
  • \(\log\) means base 10 and \(\ln\) means base \(e\).
Step 1: Log form to exponential form

The base stays the base; the value of the log becomes the exponent.

\[\log_5 625 = 4 \;\iff\; 5^4 = 625\]

\[\ln t = 2 \;\iff\; t = e^2\]

Step 2: Exponential form to log form

\[2^{-5} = \tfrac{1}{32} \;\iff\; \log_2 \tfrac{1}{32} = -5\]

Step 3: Evaluate each log by asking "what exponent?"

\[\log_2 \tfrac{1}{32} = \log_2 2^{-5} = -5\]

\[\log_9 3 = \log_9 9^{1/2} = \tfrac12 \quad (\text{since } \sqrt9 = 3)\]

(a) \(5^4 = 625\), \(t = e^2\), \(\log_2\tfrac1{32} = -5\). (b) \(-5\), \(\tfrac12\), \(-3\), \(7\).

Check

\(9^{1/2} = 3\) ✓ and \(10^{-3} = 0.001\) ✓. Notice that logs can be negative or fractions; only the input must be positive.

Example 4.5 Graphing a logarithmic function and finding an inverse

(a) Find the domain, vertical asymptote and \(x\)-intercept of \(f(x) = \log_3(x - 2) + 1\). (b) Find the inverse function \(f^{-1}\) and state its range.

(SRW, Logarithmic Functions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(f(x) = \log_3(x - 2) + 1\): \(\log_3 x\) shifted right 2 and up 1.
  • The input of a logarithm must be positive.
Step 1: Domain and asymptote

\[x - 2 \gt 0\]

\[x \gt 2\]

So the domain is \((2, \infty)\), and the vertical asymptote moves from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 2\).

Step 2: The x-intercept

Set \(f(x) = 0\) and rewrite in exponential form.

\[\log_3(x - 2) + 1 = 0\]

\[\log_3(x - 2) = -1\]

\[x - 2 = 3^{-1} = \tfrac13\]

\[x = \tfrac73\]

Step 3: Find the inverse

Let's write \(y = f(x)\), swap \(x\) and \(y\), and solve for \(y\).

\[x = \log_3(y - 2) + 1\]

\[x - 1 = \log_3(y - 2)\]

\[3^{x-1} = y - 2\]

\[y = 3^{x-1} + 2\]

Answer

(a) Domain \((2, \infty)\), vertical asymptote \(x = 2\), \(x\)-intercept \(\left(\tfrac73, 0\right)\). (b) \(f^{-1}(x) = 3^{x-1} + 2\), whose range is \((2, \infty)\), the domain of \(f\).

Check

\(f(5) = \log_3 3 + 1 = 2\), and \(f^{-1}(2) = 3^1 + 2 = 5\) ✓. The vertical asymptote \(x = 2\) of \(f\) became the horizontal asymptote \(y = 2\) of \(f^{-1}\), as the reflection in \(y = x\) predicts.

Example 4.6 Using the Laws of Logarithms

(a) Expand \(\log_b\left(\dfrac{x^2\sqrt{y}}{z^3}\right)\). (b) Combine \(2\ln x + \tfrac12\ln(x + 1) - \ln(x - 1)\) into a single logarithm. (c) Use the Change of Base Formula to find \(\log_7 50\) to four decimal places.

(SRW, Laws of Logarithms)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Product, Quotient and Power Rules (4), (5), (6).
  • Change of Base (7).
Step 1: Expand (Quotient Rule, then Product Rule, then Power Rule)

\[\log_b\left(\frac{x^2\sqrt{y}}{z^3}\right) = \log_b\left(x^2\sqrt{y}\right) - \log_b z^3\]

\[= \log_b x^2 + \log_b y^{1/2} - \log_b z^3\]

\[= 2\log_b x + \tfrac12\log_b y - 3\log_b z\]

Step 2: Combine (Power Rule first)

Let's move each coefficient up as an exponent.

\[2\ln x + \tfrac12\ln(x + 1) - \ln(x - 1) = \ln x^2 + \ln\sqrt{x + 1} - \ln(x - 1)\]

\[= \ln\left(x^2\sqrt{x + 1}\right) - \ln(x - 1)\]

\[= \ln\frac{x^2\sqrt{x + 1}}{x - 1}\]

Step 3: Change of base

\[\log_7 50 = \frac{\ln 50}{\ln 7}\]

\[= \frac{3.91202}{1.94591}\]

\[\approx 2.0104\]

Answer

(a) \(2\log_b x + \tfrac12\log_b y - 3\log_b z\). (b) \(\ln\dfrac{x^2\sqrt{x + 1}}{x - 1}\). (c) \(\log_7 50 \approx 2.0104\).

Check

\(7^2 = 49\), just below 50, so \(\log_7 50\) should be just above 2 ✓. Test (b) at \(x = 3\): \(2\ln 3 + \tfrac12\ln 4 - \ln 2 = 2\ln 3 \approx 2.197\), and \(\ln\dfrac{9 \cdot 2}{2} = \ln 9 \approx 2.197\) ✓.

Example 4.7 Solving exponential equations

Solve (a) \(3^{2x-1} = 81\), (b) \(5^x = 20\), (c) \(e^{2x} - e^x - 6 = 0\). Give decimal answers to three decimal places.

(SRW, Exponential and Logarithmic Equations)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Same base: equate exponents. Different bases: take logs.
  • \(e^x \gt 0\) for every \(x\).
Step 1: (a) Make the bases the same

\[3^{2x-1} = 3^4\]

\[2x - 1 = 4\]

\[x = \tfrac52\]

Step 2: (b) Take logs of both sides

\[\log 5^x = \log 20\]

\[x\log 5 = \log 20\]

\[x = \frac{\log 20}{\log 5} = \frac{1.30103}{0.69897} \approx 1.861\]

Step 3: (c) Substitute w = e^x

Since \(e^{2x} = (e^x)^2\), the equation is a quadratic in \(w\).

\[w^2 - w - 6 = 0\]

\[(w - 3)(w + 2) = 0\]

\[e^x = 3 \quad \text{or} \quad e^x = -2\]

Take note: \(e^x = -2\) has no solution. So

\[x = \ln 3 \approx 1.099\]

Answer

(a) \(x = \tfrac52\) (b) \(x = \log_5 20 \approx 1.861\) (c) \(x = \ln 3 \approx 1.099\).

Check

(a) \(3^{4} = 81\) ✓. (b) \(5^{1.861} \approx 19.99\) ✓ (Figure 4.7). (c) \(e^{2\ln 3} - e^{\ln 3} - 6 = 9 - 3 - 6 = 0\) ✓.

Example 4.8 Solving logarithmic equations

Solve (a) \(\log_3(x - 1) + \log_3(x + 1) = 2\), (b) \(\ln(x + 2) = 3\), (c) \(\log x + \log(x - 3) = 1\).

(SRW, Exponential and Logarithmic Equations)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Combine into one log, rewrite in exponential form, then check.
  • Every log input in the original equation must be positive.
Step 1: (a) Product Rule, then exponential form

\[\log_3[(x - 1)(x + 1)] = 2\]

\[(x - 1)(x + 1) = 3^2\]

\[x^2 - 1 = 9\]

\[x^2 = 10 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \pm\sqrt{10}\]

The logs need \(x - 1 \gt 0\), so \(x \gt 1\). Since \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16 \gt 1\) but \(-\sqrt{10} \approx -3.16 \lt 1\), we keep only \(x = \sqrt{10}\).

Step 2: (b) Exponential form with base e

\[x + 2 = e^3\]

\[x = e^3 - 2 \approx 18.086\]

Step 3: (c) Combine, then base 10

\[\log[x(x - 3)] = 1\]

\[x^2 - 3x = 10^1\]

\[x^2 - 3x - 10 = 0\]

\[(x - 5)(x + 2) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 5 \text{ or } x = -2\]

At \(x = -2\), \(\log(-2)\) is undefined, so \(-2\) is extraneous.

Answer

(a) \(x = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162\) (b) \(x = e^3 - 2 \approx 18.086\) (c) \(x = 5\).

Check

(a) \(\log_3(\sqrt{10} - 1) + \log_3(\sqrt{10} + 1) = \log_3(10 - 1) = \log_3 9 = 2\) ✓. (c) \(\log 5 + \log 2 = \log 10 = 1\) ✓.

Example 4.9 A doubling bacteria culture

A bacteria culture in an RTU laboratory starts with 200 cells and doubles every 3 hours. (a) Write a function \(P(t)\) for the number of cells after \(t\) hours. (b) Find the number after 9 hours. (c) When will the culture reach 10 000 cells?

(SRW, Modeling with Exponential Functions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Initial value \(a = 200\).
  • Growth factor 2 (it doubles) per 3-hour period, so the exponent is \(t/3\).
  • Asked: \(P(t)\), \(P(9)\), and \(t\) when \(P(t) = 10\,000\).
Step 1: Build the model

\[P(t) = 200 \cdot 2^{t/3}\]

Step 2: Evaluate at t = 9

\[P(9) = 200 \cdot 2^{9/3}\]

\[= 200 \cdot 2^3\]

\[= 200(8) = 1600\]

Step 3: Solve P(t) = 10 000

Isolate the exponential term first, then take logs.

\[200 \cdot 2^{t/3} = 10\,000\]

\[2^{t/3} = 50\]

\[\frac{t}{3}\ln 2 = \ln 50\]

\[t = \frac{3\ln 50}{\ln 2} \approx 16.93\]

Answer

(a) \(P(t) = 200 \cdot 2^{t/3}\) (b) 1600 cells (c) after about 16.9 hours.

Check

After 15 hours (5 doublings) there are \(200 \cdot 32 = 6400\) cells, and after 18 hours \(12\,800\). So 10 000 is reached between 15 and 18 hours ✓.

Example 4.10 Carbon-14 dating

Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years. (a) Find the decay rate \(r\) in \(m(t) = m_0e^{-rt}\). (b) What fraction of the carbon-14 in a piece of wood remains after 2000 years? (c) Charcoal from an old site has 30% of the carbon-14 of living wood. How old is it?

(SRW, Modeling with Exponential Functions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(h = 5730\) years, \(r = \dfrac{\ln 2}{h}\).

Step 1: The decay rate

\[r = \frac{\ln 2}{5730} \approx 0.000121 \text{ per year}\]

Step 2: Fraction after 2000 years

\[\frac{m(2000)}{m_0} = e^{-2000\ln 2/5730}\]

\[= e^{-0.24193}\]

\[\approx 0.785\]

Step 3: Age of the charcoal

\[m_0e^{-rt} = 0.30\,m_0\]

\[-rt = \ln 0.30\]

\[t = -\frac{\ln 0.30}{r} = -\frac{5730\ln 0.30}{\ln 2}\]

\[\approx 9953 \text{ years}\]

(a) \(r \approx 0.000121\) per year (b) about 78.5% remains (c) about 9950 years old.

Example 4.11 Decibels, pH and the Richter scale

(a) A jeepney engine idling produces an intensity of \(10^{-4}\) W/m². Find its sound level in decibels. (b) Find the pH of a solution with \([\text{H}^+] = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}\) M. (c) The 1990 Luzon earthquake had magnitude 7.8. How many times larger were its waves than those of a magnitude 5.8 quake?

(SRW, Logarithmic Scales)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(L = 10\log\dfrac{I}{I_0}\), \(I_0 = 10^{-12}\) W/m².
  • \(\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}^+]\).
  • \(M_1 - M_2 = \log\dfrac{A_1}{A_2}\) for two earthquakes.
Step 1: Sound level

\[L = 10\log\frac{10^{-4}}{10^{-12}}\]

\[= 10\log 10^{8}\]

\[= 10(8) = 80 \text{ dB}\]

Step 2: pH

\[\text{pH} = -\log\left(1.0 \times 10^{-4}\right)\]

\[= -(-4) = 4\]

Step 3: Comparing the earthquakes

Let's subtract the two Richter formulas; \(A_0\) cancels.

\[7.8 - 5.8 = \log\frac{A_1}{A_2}\]

\[\frac{A_1}{A_2} = 10^{2} = 100\]

Answer

(a) 80 dB (b) pH 4, an acidic solution (c) its waves were 100 times larger.

Check

Normal conversation, \(10^{-6}\) W/m², is 60 dB; the jeepney is 100 times more intense and 20 dB louder ✓. Pure water has pH 7, so pH 4 is \(10^3 = 1000\) times more acidic than water.

Example 4.12 Comparing the brightness of stars

(a) Star A is 100 times brighter than star B. How do their magnitudes compare? (b) Sirius has apparent magnitude \(-1.46\) and Polaris has \(1.98\). How many times brighter does Sirius appear?

(SRW, Logarithmic Scales)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(m_2 - m_1 = -2.5\log\dfrac{I_2}{I_1}\), equation (15).
  • (a) \(I_A/I_B = 100\). (b) \(m_S = -1.46\), \(m_P = 1.98\).

\[m_A - m_B = -2.5\log\frac{I_A}{I_B}\]

\[= -2.5\log 100\]

\[= -2.5(2) = -5\]

\[m_S - m_P = -2.5\log\frac{I_S}{I_P}\]

\[= \frac{-3.44}{-2.5} = 1.376\]

(a) Star A's magnitude is 5 lower than star B's (lower means brighter). (b) Sirius appears about 24 times brighter than Polaris.
