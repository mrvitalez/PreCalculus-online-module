1.1Real Numbers

Everything we do in precalculus (and later in calculus) happens on the real numbers. So before anything else, let's be clear about what they are and what rules they follow.

Kinds of real numbers. The natural numbers are \(1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots\). The integers are the natural numbers together with their negatives and zero: \(\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\). A rational number is a ratio of integers, \(r = \dfrac{m}{n}\) with \(n \ne 0\), such as \(\tfrac12\), \(-\tfrac37\), \(46 = \tfrac{46}{1}\) and \(0.17 = \tfrac{17}{100}\). Numbers that cannot be written as a ratio of integers are irrational, such as \(\sqrt{3}\), \(\sqrt{5}\), \(\sqrt[3]{2}\) and \(\pi\). In decimal form, a rational number either ends or repeats (\(\tfrac13 = 0.333\ldots\)); an irrational number never ends and never repeats. Figure 1.1 shows how these sets sit inside one another.

Properties of real numbers. When we add or multiply, the order does not matter (commutative), and the grouping does not matter (associative): \(7 + 3 = 3 + 7\) and \((2 + 4) + 7 = 2 + (4 + 7)\). The distributive property connects the two operations: \(2(3 + 5) = 2 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 5\). It is the property we use the most, every time we expand or factor. Can you name the property in \(4(2 \cdot 3) = (4 \cdot 2)3\)? It is the associative property of multiplication.

Subtraction undoes addition, \(a - b = a + (-b)\), and division undoes multiplication. The properties of negatives and of fractions in the box below are the rules we use without thinking; it is worth checking that you know each one.

Key properties
\[ a + b = b + a, \qquad ab = ba, \qquad a(b + c) = ab + ac \]
\[ (-a)(-b) = ab, \qquad -(a + b) = -a - b, \qquad -(a - b) = b - a \]
\[ \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}, \qquad \frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{d}{c}, \qquad \frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}, \qquad \frac{ac}{bc} = \frac{a}{b} \]

Sets. A set is a collection of objects called elements; \(a \in S\) means \(a\) is an element of \(S\). We can list the elements, \(A = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\), or use set-builder notation, \(A = \{x \mid x \text{ is an integer and } 0 \lt x \lt 7\}\), read "the set of all \(x\) such that ...". The union \(S \cup T\) contains the elements in \(S\) or \(T\); the intersection \(S \cap T\) contains the elements in both; and \(\varnothing\) is the empty set. For \(S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}\), \(T = \{4, 5, 6, 7\}\) and \(V = \{6, 7, 8\}\): \(S \cup T = \{1, 2, \ldots, 7\}\), \(S \cap T = \{4, 5\}\) and \(S \cap V = \varnothing\).

Intervals. If \(a \lt b\), the open interval \((a, b) = \{x \mid a \lt x \lt b\}\) leaves out both endpoints, and the closed interval \([a, b] = \{x \mid a \le x \le b\}\) includes them. Half-open intervals such as \((a, b]\) and infinite intervals such as \([a, \infty)\) follow the same idea (Figure 1.2). On a graph, a filled dot means "included" and an open dot means "excluded".

Absolute value and distance. The absolute value \(|a|\) is the distance from \(a\) to 0, so \(|a| = a\) if \(a \ge 0\) and \(|a| = -a\) if \(a \lt 0\). The distance between \(a\) and \(b\) on the number line is \(d(a, b) = |b - a|\). For example, the distance between \(-3\) and \(5\) is \(|5 - (-3)| = 8\).

Take note A minus sign in front of parentheses changes the sign of every term inside: \(-(x - 4) = -x + 4\), not \(-x - 4\). Also, \(|-a|\) is not always \(a\): if \(a = -2\), then \(|-a| = 2 = -a\).

1.2Exponents and Radicals

A product of identical factors is written with an exponent: \(a^n = a \cdot a \cdots a\) (\(n\) factors). The number \(a\) is the base and \(n\) is the exponent. We also define \(a^0 = 1\) and \(a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}\) for \(a \ne 0\). Notice how the parentheses matter: \((-3)^4 = 81\) but \(-3^4 = -(3^4) = -81\). And \((-2)^{-3} = \dfrac{1}{(-2)^3} = -\dfrac18\).

Laws of exponents
\[ a^m a^n = a^{m+n}, \qquad \frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}, \qquad (a^m)^n = a^{mn} \]
\[ (ab)^n = a^n b^n, \qquad \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}, \qquad \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{-n} = \left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^n, \qquad \frac{a^{-n}}{b^{-m}} = \frac{b^m}{a^n} \]

For example, \((2a^3b^2)(3ab^4)^3 = (2a^3b^2)(27a^3b^{12}) = 54a^6b^{14}\). Here we used Law 4, then Law 3, then Law 1. And \(\left(\dfrac{y}{3z^3}\right)^{-2} = \left(\dfrac{3z^3}{y}\right)^{2} = \dfrac{9z^6}{y^2}\): a negative exponent flips the fraction.

Radicals. The principal \(n\)th root is defined by \(\sqrt[n]{a} = b\) means \(b^n = a\). If \(n\) is even, we need \(a \ge 0\) and \(b \ge 0\). So \(\sqrt{9} = 3\) (not \(\pm 3\)), and \(\sqrt[3]{-8} = -2\). To simplify a root, we pull out perfect powers: \(\sqrt{32} + \sqrt{200} = \sqrt{16 \cdot 2} + \sqrt{100 \cdot 2} = 4\sqrt2 + 10\sqrt2 = 14\sqrt2\).

Rational exponents. We define \(a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}\), and for \(m/n\) in lowest terms, \(a^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m = \sqrt[n]{a^m}\). All the laws of exponents still work. So \(8^{2/3} = (\sqrt[3]{8})^2 = 4\), and \(\sqrt{x\sqrt{x}} = (x \cdot x^{1/2})^{1/2} = (x^{3/2})^{1/2} = x^{3/4}\).

Properties of nth roots
\[ \sqrt[n]{ab} = \sqrt[n]{a}\,\sqrt[n]{b}, \qquad \sqrt[n]{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}}, \qquad \sqrt[m]{\sqrt[n]{a}} = \sqrt[mn]{a} \]
\[ \sqrt[n]{a^n} = a \;\;(n \text{ odd}), \qquad \sqrt[n]{a^n} = |a| \;\;(n \text{ even}), \qquad a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} \]

Rationalizing the denominator. A fraction whose denominator has no radicals is in standard form. To get there, we multiply by a clever form of 1. For a square root: \(\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt3} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{2\sqrt3}{3}\). For a cube root we need a perfect cube: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{5}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{5}} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt[3]{5^2}}{\sqrt[3]{5^2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt[3]{25}}{5}\).

Take note Exponents and roots distribute over products, not sums. \((a + b)^2 \ne a^2 + b^2\) and \(\sqrt{a + b} \ne \sqrt a + \sqrt b\). Try \(a = 9\), \(b = 16\): \(\sqrt{25} = 5\), but \(\sqrt9 + \sqrt{16} = 7\). Also remember \(\sqrt{x^2} = |x|\), not \(x\).

1.3Algebraic Expressions

A polynomial in \(x\) is an expression \(a_nx^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1x + a_0\), where the coefficients \(a_k\) are real numbers and \(n\) is a nonnegative integer. The pieces \(a_kx^k\) are its terms, and if \(a_n \ne 0\) the polynomial has degree \(n\). So \(3x^4 - x + 7\) has degree 4, while \(x^{-1} + 2\) and \(\sqrt{x}\) are not polynomials.

Adding and multiplying. We add and subtract by combining like terms: \((x^3 - 6x^2 + 2x + 4) - (x^3 + 5x^2 - 7x) = -11x^2 + 9x + 4\). Take note that the minus sign goes to every term of the second polynomial. We multiply with the distributive property, \((a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd\), which for two binomials is the FOIL method: \((2x + 1)(3x - 5) = 6x^2 - 10x + 3x - 5 = 6x^2 - 7x - 5\).

Special products and factoring formulas
\[ (A + B)(A - B) = A^2 - B^2 \]
\[ (A \pm B)^2 = A^2 \pm 2AB + B^2, \qquad (A \pm B)^3 = A^3 \pm 3A^2B + 3AB^2 \pm B^3 \]
\[ A^3 - B^3 = (A - B)(A^2 + AB + B^2), \qquad A^3 + B^3 = (A + B)(A^2 - AB + B^2) \]

Factoring is multiplication in reverse. Let's always look for a common factor first: \(8x^4y^2 + 6x^3y^3 - 2xy^4 = 2xy^2(4x^3 + 3x^2y - y^2)\). For a trinomial \(x^2 + bx + c\), we need two numbers \(r\) and \(s\) with \(r + s = b\) and \(rs = c\): \(x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)\). For \(ax^2 + bx + c\) with \(a \ne 1\), we use trial and error with \((px + r)(qx + s)\): \(6x^2 + 7x - 5 = (3x + 5)(2x - 1)\).

Then we look for the special forms: \(4x^2 - 25 = (2x + 5)(2x - 5)\), \(4x^2 - 4xy + y^2 = (2x - y)^2\), \(27x^3 - 1 = (3x - 1)(9x^2 + 3x + 1)\). Polynomials with four terms can often be factored by grouping: \(x^3 + x^2 + 4x + 4 = x^2(x + 1) + 4(x + 1) = (x^2 + 4)(x + 1)\). And a whole expression can play the role of \(x\): if we let \(u = 5a + 1\), then \((5a + 1)^2 - 2(5a + 1) - 3 = u^2 - 2u - 3 = (u + 1)(u - 3) = (5a + 2)(5a - 2)\).

Expressions with fractional exponents factor the same way: we take out the smallest power. \(3x^{3/2} - 9x^{1/2} + 6x^{-1/2} = 3x^{-1/2}(x^2 - 3x + 2) = 3x^{-1/2}(x - 1)(x - 2)\).

Take note "Factor" means factor completely. \(2x^4 - 8x^2 = 2x^2(x^2 - 4)\) is not finished; it is \(2x^2(x + 2)(x - 2)\). On the other hand, a sum of squares such as \(x^2 + 4\) does not factor over the real numbers, so stop there.

1.4Rational Expressions

A rational expression is a fraction whose numerator and denominator are polynomials, like \(\dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 + x - 2}\). Since we cannot divide by zero, every algebraic expression comes with a domain: the set of real numbers the variable is allowed to take.

For example, \(2x^2 + 3x - 1\) is defined for every real \(x\). But \(\dfrac{x}{x^2 - 5x + 6} = \dfrac{x}{(x - 2)(x - 3)}\) has domain \(\{x \mid x \ne 2 \text{ and } x \ne 3\}\). And \(\dfrac{\sqrt{x}}{x - 5}\) needs \(x \ge 0\) for the root and \(x \ne 5\) for the denominator.

Simplifying, multiplying and dividing. We factor everything first, then cancel common factors using \(\dfrac{AC}{BC} = \dfrac{A}{B}\):

\[ \frac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 + x - 2} = \frac{(x + 1)(x - 1)}{(x + 2)(x - 1)} = \frac{x + 1}{x + 2} \]

To multiply, we multiply numerators and denominators; to divide, we multiply by the reciprocal of the divisor. Then we factor and cancel as before.

Adding and subtracting. We need a common denominator, and the least common denominator (LCD) keeps the work small. Factor each denominator, and take each factor the greatest number of times it appears:

\[ \frac{1}{x^2 - 1} - \frac{2}{(x + 1)^2} = \frac{(x + 1) - 2(x - 1)}{(x - 1)(x + 1)^2} = \frac{3 - x}{(x - 1)(x + 1)^2} \]

Compound fractions have fractions inside the numerator or denominator. One clean method: combine the top into one fraction, combine the bottom into one fraction, then divide. The difference quotient of \(1/x\) is the most important example for calculus:

\[ \frac{\dfrac{1}{a + h} - \dfrac{1}{a}}{h} = \frac{a - (a + h)}{a(a + h)} \cdot \frac{1}{h} = \frac{-h}{a(a + h)h} = -\frac{1}{a(a + h)} \]

Conjugates. To rationalize a denominator of the form \(A + B\sqrt{C}\), we multiply top and bottom by its conjugate radical \(A - B\sqrt{C}\), because \((A + B\sqrt C)(A - B\sqrt C) = A^2 - B^2C\) has no root. Sometimes, in calculus, we rationalize the numerator instead: \(\dfrac{\sqrt{4 + h} - 2}{h} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{4 + h} + 2}{\sqrt{4 + h} + 2} = \dfrac{(4 + h) - 4}{h(\sqrt{4 + h} + 2)} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4 + h} + 2}\).

Key equations
\[ \frac{A}{B} \cdot \frac{C}{D} = \frac{AC}{BD}, \qquad \frac{A}{B} \div \frac{C}{D} = \frac{A}{B} \cdot \frac{D}{C}, \qquad \frac{A}{C} + \frac{B}{C} = \frac{A + B}{C} \]
\[ \frac{1}{1 + \sqrt2} = \frac{1}{1 + \sqrt2} \cdot \frac{1 - \sqrt2}{1 - \sqrt2} = \frac{1 - \sqrt2}{1 - 2} = \sqrt2 - 1 \]
Take note We cancel factors, never terms. In \(\dfrac{x + 2}{x}\) the \(x\) is a term of the numerator, so \(\dfrac{x + 2}{x} \ne 2\) (try \(x = 1\): we get 3). Also, \(\dfrac{a}{b + c} \ne \dfrac{a}{b} + \dfrac{a}{c}\).

1.5Equations

An equation is a statement that two expressions are equal; to solve it is to find every value of the variable that makes it true. We get equivalent equations (same solutions) by adding the same quantity to both sides, \(A = B \Leftrightarrow A + C = B + C\), or by multiplying both sides by the same nonzero quantity, \(A = B \Leftrightarrow CA = CB\) (\(C \ne 0\)).

Linear equations are equivalent to \(ax + b = 0\). For \(7x - 4 = 3x + 8\), we subtract \(3x\) and add 4 to get \(4x = 12\), so \(x = 3\). The same steps let us solve a formula for one of its letters. In physics, \(v = v_0 + at\) solved for \(t\) gives \(t = \dfrac{v - v_0}{a}\). Equations such as \(x^2 + 2x = 8\), \(\sqrt{x} - 6x = 0\) and \(\dfrac{3}{x} - 2x = 1\) are not linear.

Quadratic equations have the form \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), \(a \ne 0\). There are four tools:

  • Factoring, using the zero-product property (\(AB = 0\) means \(A = 0\) or \(B = 0\)): \(x^2 + 5x = 24 \Rightarrow (x + 8)(x - 3) = 0 \Rightarrow x = -8\) or \(x = 3\).
  • Square roots: \(x^2 = c\) gives \(x = \pm\sqrt{c}\). So \((x - 4)^2 = 5\) gives \(x = 4 \pm \sqrt5\).
  • Completing the square: add \(\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2\) to make \(x^2 + bx\) a perfect square. For \(x^2 - 8x + 13 = 0\): \(x^2 - 8x + 16 = -13 + 16\), so \((x - 4)^2 = 3\) and \(x = 4 \pm \sqrt3\).
  • The quadratic formula, which works every time.

Where does the formula come from? It is completing the square done once, in general. Starting from \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), divide by \(a\) and move \(c/a\):

\[ x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a} \;\Rightarrow\; x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{b^2}{4a^2} = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a} \;\Rightarrow\; \left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2} \]

Taking square roots, \(x + \dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\), and subtracting \(\dfrac{b}{2a}\) gives the formula in the box.

Key equations
\[ x^2 + bx + \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 \]
\[ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}, \qquad D = b^2 - 4ac \]
\[ D \gt 0: \text{two real solutions}, \quad D = 0: \text{one real solution}, \quad D \lt 0: \text{no real solution} \]

For \(3x^2 - 5x - 1 = 0\): \(x = \dfrac{5 \pm \sqrt{25 + 12}}{6} = \dfrac{5 \pm \sqrt{37}}{6}\). The discriminant \(D = b^2 - 4ac\) tells us how many real solutions to expect before we solve (Figure 1.3): \(x^2 + 4x - 1 = 0\) has \(D = 20 \gt 0\), two solutions; \(4x^2 + 12x + 9 = 0\) has \(D = 0\), one solution \(x = -\tfrac32\); \(\tfrac13x^2 - 2x + 4 = 0\) has \(D = -\tfrac43 \lt 0\), no real solution.

Other equations. A rational equation is cleared of fractions by multiplying by the LCD. A radical equation is solved by isolating the root and raising both sides to a power. An equation of quadratic type, like \(x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = 0\), becomes a quadratic with \(u = x^2\). These steps can create extraneous solutions, so we must check every answer in the original equation.

Modeling. In a word problem, let's (1) name the unknown, (2) translate the words into an equation (for motion problems, use distance = rate × time), (3) solve, and (4) check that the answer makes sense (a speed cannot be negative). See Example 1.6.

Take note Never divide both sides by an expression that could be zero. From \(x^2 = 3x\), dividing by \(x\) gives only \(x = 3\) and loses \(x = 0\). Instead, write \(x^2 - 3x = 0\), so \(x(x - 3) = 0\), and \(x = 0\) or \(x = 3\).

1.6Complex Numbers

The equation \(x^2 = -1\) has no real solution, because the square of a real number is never negative. So we invent a number for it: the imaginary unit \(i\), with \(i^2 = -1\). A complex number has the form \(a + bi\), where \(a\) is the real part and \(b\) is the imaginary part. Two complex numbers are equal only if both parts are equal. The real numbers are the complex numbers with \(b = 0\).

Arithmetic. We add, subtract and multiply exactly like binomials, replacing \(i^2\) by \(-1\):

\[ (3 + 5i) + (4 - 2i) = 7 + 3i, \qquad (3 + 5i) - (4 - 2i) = -1 + 7i \]
\[ (3 + 5i)(4 - 2i) = 12 - 6i + 20i - 10i^2 = 12 + 14i + 10 = 22 + 14i \]

Powers of \(i\) repeat in a cycle of four: \(i, -1, -i, 1\). So \(i^{23} = i^{20} \cdot i^3 = (i^4)^5 \cdot (-i) = -i\).

Division. The complex conjugate of \(z = a + bi\) is \(\bar z = a - bi\), and \(z\bar z = a^2 + b^2\) is real. So to divide, we multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator:

\[ \frac{3 + 5i}{1 - 2i} = \frac{(3 + 5i)(1 + 2i)}{(1 - 2i)(1 + 2i)} = \frac{3 + 6i + 5i + 10i^2}{1 + 4} = \frac{-7 + 11i}{5} = -\frac75 + \frac{11}{5}i \]

It helps to picture \(a + bi\) as the point \((a, b)\) in the complex plane (Figure 1.4). Adding complex numbers then works like adding arrows, and the conjugate is the mirror image in the real axis.

Square roots of negative numbers. If \(r \gt 0\), then \(\sqrt{-r} = i\sqrt{r}\). So \(\sqrt{-16} = 4i\) and \(\sqrt{-3} = i\sqrt3\). Now every quadratic has solutions: when \(D \lt 0\) the quadratic formula gives a pair of complex conjugates. For \(x^2 + 4x + 5 = 0\): \(x = \dfrac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 20}}{2} = \dfrac{-4 \pm 2i}{2} = -2 \pm i\).

Key equations
\[ i^2 = -1, \qquad (a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i \]
\[ \frac{a + bi}{c + di} = \frac{(a + bi)(c - di)}{c^2 + d^2}, \qquad \sqrt{-r} = i\sqrt{r} \;\;(r \gt 0) \]
Take note The rule \(\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}\) fails when both numbers are negative. \(\sqrt{-4}\,\sqrt{-9} = (2i)(3i) = 6i^2 = -6\), but \(\sqrt{(-4)(-9)} = \sqrt{36} = 6\). Always write \(\sqrt{-r}\) as \(i\sqrt r\) first, then multiply.

1.7Inequalities

An inequality looks like an equation with \(\lt, \gt, \le\) or \(\ge\) in place of \(=\). Its solutions usually form an interval or a union of intervals, not a few numbers. We solve it with almost the same rules as equations, with one big exception.

Rules for inequalities
\[ A \le B \;\Leftrightarrow\; A + C \le B + C \]
\[ C \gt 0: \; A \le B \Leftrightarrow CA \le CB, \qquad C \lt 0: \; A \le B \Leftrightarrow CA \ge CB \]
\[ A, B \gt 0: \; A \le B \Leftrightarrow \frac1A \ge \frac1B \]

Linear inequalities. For \(3x \lt 9x + 4\): \(-6x \lt 4\). Dividing by \(-6\) reverses the inequality, so \(x \gt -\tfrac23\); the solution is \(\left(-\tfrac23, \infty\right)\). A compound inequality is solved on all three parts at once: \(4 \le 3x - 2 \lt 13 \Rightarrow 6 \le 3x \lt 15 \Rightarrow 2 \le x \lt 5\), that is, \([2, 5)\).

Nonlinear inequalities and sign charts. Here is the method:

  1. Move everything to one side, so the other side is 0.
  2. Factor (for a fraction, factor the numerator and denominator).
  3. Find the numbers where a factor is 0. They cut the line into intervals.
  4. Make a sign chart: the sign of each factor on each interval, then the sign of the product.
  5. Read off the answer; include endpoints only for \(\le\) or \(\ge\), and never where a denominator is 0.

For \(x(x - 1)(x - 3) \lt 0\) the cut points are 0, 1 and 3, and Figure 1.5 shows that the product is negative on \((-\infty, 0) \cup (1, 3)\). For \(x^2 \le 5x - 6\): \(x^2 - 5x + 6 \le 0\), so \((x - 2)(x - 3) \le 0\), which holds on \([2, 3]\).

For a rational inequality such as \(\dfrac{1 + x}{1 - x} \ge 1\), we subtract 1 and combine: \(\dfrac{1 + x - (1 - x)}{1 - x} = \dfrac{2x}{1 - x} \ge 0\). The cut points are 0 and 1, and the sign chart gives \([0, 1)\). Notice that 1 is excluded because the denominator is zero there.

Absolute-value inequalities. Since \(|x|\) is the distance from 0, \(|x| \lt c\) means "closer than \(c\) to 0" and \(|x| \gt c\) means "farther than \(c\)". For \(|x - 5| \lt 2\): \(-2 \lt x - 5 \lt 2\), so \(3 \lt x \lt 7\), the numbers within 2 units of 5. For \(|3x + 2| \ge 4\): \(3x + 2 \ge 4\) or \(3x + 2 \le -4\), so \(x \ge \tfrac23\) or \(x \le -2\), that is, \((-\infty, -2] \cup \left[\tfrac23, \infty\right)\).

Absolute-value inequalities (c > 0)
\[ |x| \lt c \;\Leftrightarrow\; -c \lt x \lt c, \qquad |x| \le c \;\Leftrightarrow\; -c \le x \le c \]
\[ |x| \gt c \;\Leftrightarrow\; x \lt -c \text{ or } x \gt c, \qquad |x| \ge c \;\Leftrightarrow\; x \le -c \text{ or } x \ge c \]
Take note Do not multiply both sides of \(\dfrac{1 + x}{1 - x} \ge 1\) by \(1 - x\): we do not know whether it is positive or negative, so we do not know whether to flip the inequality. Move everything to one side and use a sign chart instead.

1.8The Coordinate Plane; Graphs of Equations; Circles

The coordinate plane joins algebra and geometry: every point is an ordered pair \((x, y)\), and every equation in \(x\) and \(y\) becomes a picture. The axes divide the plane into four quadrants.

Distance and midpoint. The distance between \(A(x_1, y_1)\) and \(B(x_2, y_2)\) comes from the Pythagorean Theorem: the horizontal and vertical legs are \(|x_2 - x_1|\) and \(|y_2 - y_1|\) (Figure 1.6). The midpoint is found by averaging the coordinates. For \(A(-2, 1)\) and \(B(4, 9)\): \(d = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10\) and \(M = \left(\tfrac{-2 + 4}{2}, \tfrac{1 + 9}{2}\right) = (1, 5)\).

Graphs of equations. The graph of an equation is the set of all points \((x, y)\) that satisfy it. We can sketch it by plotting points, but two shortcuts help a lot:

  • Intercepts. To find \(x\)-intercepts, set \(y = 0\) and solve for \(x\); to find \(y\)-intercepts, set \(x = 0\) and solve for \(y\). For \(y = x^2 - 4\): the \(x\)-intercepts are \(\pm2\) and the \(y\)-intercept is \(-4\).
  • Symmetry. If replacing \(y\) by \(-y\) gives the same equation, the graph is symmetric about the \(x\)-axis. Replacing \(x\) by \(-x\) tests the \(y\)-axis. Replacing both tests the origin. Since \((-x)^2 - 4 = x^2 - 4\), the graph of \(y = x^2 - 4\) is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis.

Circles. A circle is the set of points at distance \(r\) from a centre \((h, k)\). Writing that distance with the distance formula and squaring gives the standard form in the box. If the equation is given in expanded form, we complete the square in \(x\) and in \(y\). For \(x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y - 4 = 0\):

\[ (x^2 - 4x + 4) + (y^2 + 2y + 1) = 4 + 4 + 1 \;\Rightarrow\; (x - 2)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 9 \]

So the centre is \((2, -1)\) and the radius is 3 (Figure 1.7). Setting \(y = 0\) gives \((x - 2)^2 = 8\), so the \(x\)-intercepts are \(2 \pm 2\sqrt2\).

Key equations
\[ d(A, B) = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}, \qquad M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right) \]
\[ (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2 \quad \text{(circle, centre } (h, k), \text{ radius } r) \]
Take note Watch the signs in the standard form. \((x + 3)^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 16\) means \(h = -3\), \(k = 2\), so the centre is \((-3, 2)\), not \((3, -2)\). And the radius is \(\sqrt{16} = 4\), not 16. If completing the square gives a negative number on the right, there is no circle at all.

1.9Lines

The slope of a nonvertical line through \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) is "rise over run", \(m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\). A positive slope means the line rises to the right, a negative slope means it falls, a horizontal line has slope 0, and a vertical line has no slope (undefined). For the points \((2, 1)\) and \((5, 7)\): \(m = \dfrac{7 - 1}{5 - 2} = 2\), so \(y\) goes up 2 units for each unit to the right.

Equations of lines. If we know a point and the slope, the point-slope form gives the line immediately. Solving it for \(y\) gives the slope-intercept form \(y = mx + b\), where \(b\) is the \(y\)-intercept. Every line, vertical lines included, can be written in general form \(Ax + By + C = 0\). For example, the line through \((2, 1)\) with slope 2 is \(y - 1 = 2(x - 2)\), that is, \(y = 2x - 3\), or \(2x - y - 3 = 0\). To read the slope from \(3x - 4y = 8\), solve for \(y\): \(y = \tfrac34x - 2\), so \(m = \tfrac34\).

Key equations
\[ m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}, \qquad y - y_1 = m(x - x_1), \qquad y = mx + b \]
\[ x = a \;\text{(vertical)}, \qquad y = b \;\text{(horizontal)}, \qquad Ax + By + C = 0 \;\text{(general)} \]
\[ \text{parallel: } m_1 = m_2, \qquad \text{perpendicular: } m_1 m_2 = -1 \;\left(m_2 = -\frac{1}{m_1}\right) \]

Parallel and perpendicular lines. Parallel lines have the same slope. Perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals. Let's find both lines through \(P(-1, 3)\) related to \(y = 2x - 1\) (Figure 1.8). The parallel line has slope 2: \(y - 3 = 2(x + 1)\), so \(y = 2x + 5\). The perpendicular line has slope \(-\tfrac12\): \(y - 3 = -\tfrac12(x + 1)\), so \(y = -\tfrac12x + \tfrac52\).

Slope as a rate of change. In applications the slope is a rate: pesos per kilometre, metres per second, degrees per hour. If a load of rice costs \(C = 52w + 10\) pesos for \(w\) kilograms (with a ₱10 bag fee), the slope 52 is the price per kilogram and the intercept 10 is the fixed cost. Example 1.11 builds such a model from two data points.

Take note Keep the order the same on top and bottom of the slope formula: \(\dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\), not \(\dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_1 - x_2}\). And "perpendicular" means negative reciprocal: the perpendicular to a line of slope 2 has slope \(-\tfrac12\), not \(-2\).

1.10Solving Equations and Inequalities Graphically

Some equations, like \(x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0\), have no easy algebraic method. A graph (on Desmos, GeoGebra or a graphing calculator) still lets us find the solutions, at least approximately. There are two ways to set this up.

  • Intersection method. To solve \(f(x) = g(x)\), graph \(y = f(x)\) and \(y = g(x)\) on the same axes. The solutions are the \(x\)-coordinates of the points where the graphs meet.
  • Zero method. Move everything to one side, \(f(x) - g(x) = 0\), and graph \(y = f(x) - g(x)\). The solutions are its \(x\)-intercepts.

Inequalities. To solve \(f(x) \le g(x)\) graphically, we look for the \(x\)-values where the graph of \(f\) is below (or touching) the graph of \(g\). For \(x^2 \le x + 6\), the parabola \(y = x^2\) is below the line \(y = x + 6\) between their intersection points \((-2, 4)\) and \((3, 9)\), so the solution is \([-2, 3]\) (Figure 1.9). Algebra confirms it: \(x^2 - x - 6 = (x + 2)(x - 3) \le 0\) on \([-2, 3]\).

Zooming in. A graphing tool gives decimal answers, and we can make them as accurate as we like by zooming in on an intercept. For \(y = x^3 - 2x - 5\): \(y(2) = -1 \lt 0\) and \(y(2.1) \approx 0.061 \gt 0\), so the graph crosses the \(x\)-axis between 2 and 2.1. Zooming further shows \(x \approx 2.09\) (Example 1.12).

Algebraic or graphical? The algebraic method gives exact answers, like \(4 + \sqrt3\). The graphical method works for almost any equation, gives a picture of all the solutions, and is a great way to check algebra. In practice, let's use both.

Key equations
\[ f(x) = g(x) \;\Leftrightarrow\; f(x) - g(x) = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; x \text{ is an } x\text{-intercept of } y = f(x) - g(x) \]
\[ f(x) \le g(x) \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{the graph of } f \text{ is on or below the graph of } g \]
Take note A viewing rectangle can hide solutions. If we graph \(y = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x - 3\) only for \(0 \le x \le 3\), we see two roots and miss the third one near \(x = 3.88\). Always check the overall shape with a wide window first, then zoom in. And remember that graphical answers are approximate: \(2.09\) is not the exact root.

★Key takeaways

  • The real numbers are the rational and irrational numbers together; the commutative, associative and distributive properties justify every algebra step we take.
  • Intervals use [ ] for included endpoints and ( ) for excluded ones; ∞ always gets a parenthesis. Union means "or", intersection means "and".
  • The laws of exponents work for integer and rational exponents; a^(m/n) is the nth root of a^m, and √(x²) = |x|. Rationalize with a clever form of 1 or with the conjugate.
  • Factor completely: common factor first, then trinomials, special formulas and grouping. Cancel factors, never terms, and state the domain of a rational expression.
  • Quadratics: factor, take square roots, complete the square or use x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a). The discriminant D = b² − 4ac counts the real solutions. Check rational and radical equations for extraneous solutions.
  • Complex numbers a + bi follow the usual algebra with i² = −1; divide with the conjugate, and write √(−r) = i√r before multiplying.
  • Inequalities: flip the sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative; for nonlinear inequalities move everything to one side, factor, and use a sign chart.
  • Distance, midpoint, circles (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r², and lines y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) connect algebra to pictures, and a graph lets us solve f(x) = g(x) or f(x) ≤ g(x) when algebra gets hard.

Example 1.1 Intervals, unions and intersections

Let \(I = (-3, 4]\) and \(J = [1, \infty)\). Write \(I\) and \(J\) in set-builder notation, then find \(I \cap J\) and \(I \cup J\).

(SRW, Real Numbers)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(I = (-3, 4]\): \(-3\) excluded, 4 included.
  • \(J = [1, \infty)\): 1 included, no right endpoint.
  • Asked: set-builder forms, \(I \cap J\) and \(I \cup J\).
Step 1: Translate to inequalities

A parenthesis gives a strict inequality, a bracket gives \(\le\).

\[I = \{x \mid -3 \lt x \le 4\}\]

\[J = \{x \mid x \ge 1\}\]

Step 2: Intersection (numbers in both)

We need \(-3 \lt x \le 4\) and \(x \ge 1\). The stronger left condition is \(x \ge 1\), and the stronger right condition is \(x \le 4\).

\[I \cap J = \{x \mid 1 \le x \le 4\} = [1, 4]\]

Step 3: Union (numbers in either)

\(I\) covers everything from just above \(-3\) up to 4, and \(J\) continues from 1 to infinity. Together there is no gap.

\[I \cup J = \{x \mid x \gt -3\} = (-3, \infty)\]

Answer

\(I = \{x \mid -3 \lt x \le 4\}\), \(J = \{x \mid x \ge 1\}\), \(I \cap J = [1, 4]\) and \(I \cup J = (-3, \infty)\).

Check

Test \(x = 4\): it is in \(I\) (bracket) and in \(J\), so it belongs to \(I \cap J\), as the bracket in \([1, 4]\) says. Test \(x = -3\): in neither set, so it is not in the union. A common slip is to write \((-3, 4]\) for the union and forget the part of \(J\) beyond 4.

Example 1.2 Exponents, radicals and a cube-root denominator

Simplify, writing answers with positive exponents: (a) \(\dfrac{6st^{-4}}{2s^{-2}t^2}\); (b) \(\left(2a^3b^4\right)^{3/2}\) for \(a, b \gt 0\). (c) Rationalize the denominator of \(\dfrac{7}{\sqrt[3]{a^2}}\).

(SRW, Exponents and Radicals)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Three expressions with negative exponents, a rational exponent and a cube root.
  • Asked: simplify (a) and (b); put (c) in standard form.
Step 1: (a) Move negative exponents, then use Law 2

A factor with a negative exponent moves across the fraction bar and its exponent becomes positive.

\[\frac{6st^{-4}}{2s^{-2}t^2} = \frac{6s \cdot s^2}{2t^2 \cdot t^4}\]

\[= \frac{6s^3}{2t^6}\]

\[= \frac{3s^3}{t^6}\]

Step 2: (b) Use Laws 4 and 3

Raise each factor to the power \(3/2\), multiplying the exponents.

\[\left(2a^3b^4\right)^{3/2} = 2^{3/2}a^{9/2}b^{6}\]

Take note that \(2^{3/2} = \sqrt{2^3} = 2\sqrt2\), and \(a^{9/2} = a^4\sqrt{a}\).

\[= 2\sqrt2\,a^4\sqrt{a}\,b^6 = 2a^4b^6\sqrt{2a}\]

Step 3: (c) Make a perfect cube under the root

We have \(\sqrt[3]{a^2}\); one more factor of \(a\) gives \(\sqrt[3]{a^3} = a\). So we multiply by \(\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{a}}{\sqrt[3]{a}}\).

\[\frac{7}{\sqrt[3]{a^2}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt[3]{a}}{\sqrt[3]{a}} = \frac{7\sqrt[3]{a}}{\sqrt[3]{a^3}}\]

\[= \frac{7\sqrt[3]{a}}{a}\]

Answer

(a) \(\dfrac{3s^3}{t^6}\); (b) \(2^{3/2}a^{9/2}b^6 = 2a^4b^6\sqrt{2a}\); (c) \(\dfrac{7\sqrt[3]{a}}{a}\).

Check

For (a), put \(s = 1\), \(t = 1\): the original is \(\tfrac62 = 3\) and the answer is 3. For (c), with \(a = 8\): \(\tfrac{7}{\sqrt[3]{64}} = \tfrac74\) and \(\tfrac{7 \cdot 2}{8} = \tfrac74\). A common mistake in (c) is to multiply by \(\sqrt[3]{a^2}/\sqrt[3]{a^2}\), which gives \(\sqrt[3]{a^4}\), still not a perfect cube.

Example 1.3 Factoring by grouping and by substitution

Factor completely: (a) \(4x^3 + 8x^2 - 9x - 18\); (b) \((2x - 1)^2 - 3(2x - 1) - 4\).

(SRW, Algebraic Expressions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • (a) four terms, no common factor for all four.
  • (b) the block \(2x - 1\) appears twice, like a trinomial.
  • Asked: factor completely.
Step 1: (a) Group in pairs

Take a common factor out of the first two terms and out of the last two terms.

\[4x^3 + 8x^2 - 9x - 18 = 4x^2(x + 2) - 9(x + 2)\]

\[= (4x^2 - 9)(x + 2)\]

Step 2: (a) Finish with a difference of squares

\(4x^2 - 9 = (2x)^2 - 3^2\), so it factors further.

\[= (2x - 3)(2x + 3)(x + 2)\]

Step 3: (b) Substitute

Let \(u = 2x - 1\). The expression becomes a simple trinomial: two numbers with sum \(-3\) and product \(-4\) are \(-4\) and 1.

\[u^2 - 3u - 4 = (u - 4)(u + 1)\]

Step 4: (b) Substitute back and simplify

\[= \big((2x - 1) - 4\big)\big((2x - 1) + 1\big)\]

\[= (2x - 5)(2x)\]

\[= 2x(2x - 5)\]

Answer

(a) \((2x - 3)(2x + 3)(x + 2)\); (b) \(2x(2x - 5)\).

Check

Put \(x = 1\) in (a): \(4 + 8 - 9 - 18 = -15\), and \((-1)(5)(3) = -15\). In (b): \(1 - 3 - 4 = -6\), and \(2(1)(-3) = -6\). Both match.

Example 1.4 Subtracting rational expressions

Simplify \(\dfrac{2}{x^2 - 4} - \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 2x}\) and state the values of \(x\) that must be excluded.

(SRW, Rational Expressions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Two rational expressions with different denominators.
  • Asked: a single simplified fraction and its domain.
Step 1: Factor the denominators

\[x^2 - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2), \qquad x^2 + 2x = x(x + 2)\]

From this, we know that \(x \ne 2\), \(x \ne -2\) and \(x \ne 0\).

Step 2: Build the LCD

Take each factor the most times it appears: \(x(x - 2)(x + 2)\).

\[\frac{2}{(x - 2)(x + 2)} - \frac{1}{x(x + 2)} = \frac{2x}{x(x - 2)(x + 2)} - \frac{x - 2}{x(x - 2)(x + 2)}\]

Step 3: Subtract the numerators

Take note of the parentheses: we subtract the whole numerator \(x - 2\).

\[= \frac{2x - (x - 2)}{x(x - 2)(x + 2)}\]

\[= \frac{x + 2}{x(x - 2)(x + 2)}\]

Step 4: Cancel the common factor

\[= \frac{1}{x(x - 2)}\]

Answer

\(\dfrac{1}{x(x - 2)}\), for \(x \ne 0, 2, -2\). (The value \(x = -2\) stays excluded even though its factor cancelled, because the original expression is undefined there.)

Check

At \(x = 1\): \(\tfrac{2}{-3} - \tfrac13 = -1\), and \(\tfrac{1}{1(-1)} = -1\). If we had written \(2x - x - 2\) in Step 3, we would get \(\tfrac{x - 2}{\ldots}\), a wrong answer.

Example 1.5 Completing the square; a radical equation

(a) Solve \(2x^2 - 8x + 3 = 0\) by completing the square. (b) Solve \(\sqrt{2x + 1} + 1 = x\).

(SRW, Equations)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • (a) a quadratic with leading coefficient 2.
  • (b) an equation with a square root.
  • Asked: all real solutions.
Step 1: (a) Make the leading coefficient 1

Move the constant and divide by 2.

\[2x^2 - 8x = -3\]

\[x^2 - 4x = -\frac32\]

Step 2: (a) Add \((b/2)^2 = (-2)^2 = 4\) to both sides

\[x^2 - 4x + 4 = -\frac32 + 4\]

\[(x - 2)^2 = \frac52\]

\[x - 2 = \pm\sqrt{\frac52} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\]

\[x = 2 \pm \frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\]

Step 3: (b) Isolate the root and square

\[\sqrt{2x + 1} = x - 1\]

\[2x + 1 = x^2 - 2x + 1\]

\[0 = x^2 - 4x = x(x - 4)\]

So the candidates are \(x = 0\) and \(x = 4\).

Step 4: (b) Check in the original equation

Squaring can create extraneous solutions, so let's test both.

\[x = 0: \; \sqrt{1} + 1 = 2 \ne 0 \quad \text{(reject)}\]

\[x = 4: \; \sqrt{9} + 1 = 4 = 4 \quad \text{(keep)}\]

Answer

(a) \(x = 2 \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\) (about 3.581 and 0.419). (b) \(x = 4\) only.

Check

For (a), the quadratic formula gives \(x = \dfrac{8 \pm \sqrt{64 - 24}}{4} = \dfrac{8 \pm 2\sqrt{10}}{4} = 2 \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\). Same answer.

Example 1.6 A round trip from Manila

Ana drives from Manila to a town 200 km away. On the way back, with lighter traffic, her average speed is 10 km/h faster. The whole trip takes 9 hours of driving. Find her average speed on the way there.

(SRW, Equations: modeling with equations)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Distance each way: 200 km.
  • Speed going: \(r\) km/h; speed returning: \(r + 10\) km/h.
  • Total driving time: 9 h.
  • Asked: \(r\).
Step 1: Write each time as distance over rate

Since distance = rate × time, time = distance ÷ rate.

\[t_{\text{going}} = \frac{200}{r}, \qquad t_{\text{back}} = \frac{200}{r + 10}\]

Step 2: Set up the equation

\[\frac{200}{r} + \frac{200}{r + 10} = 9\]

Step 3: Clear the fractions (multiply by \(r(r + 10)\))

\[200(r + 10) + 200r = 9r(r + 10)\]

\[400r + 2000 = 9r^2 + 90r\]

\[9r^2 - 310r - 2000 = 0\]

Step 4: Use the quadratic formula

\[r = \frac{310 \pm \sqrt{310^2 + 4(9)(2000)}}{18} = \frac{310 \pm \sqrt{168100}}{18} = \frac{310 \pm 410}{18}\]

\[r = 40 \quad \text{or} \quad r = -\frac{50}{9}\]

A speed cannot be negative, so we reject \(-\tfrac{50}{9}\).

Answer

Ana's average speed going was 40 km/h (and 50 km/h coming back).

Check

\(\tfrac{200}{40} + \tfrac{200}{50} = 5 + 4 = 9\) hours. ✓

Example 1.7 Dividing complex numbers; complex roots

(a) Write \(\dfrac{2 - 3i}{1 + 2i}\) in the form \(a + bi\). (b) Solve \(x^2 - 6x + 13 = 0\).

(SRW, Complex Numbers)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • (a) a quotient of complex numbers; the conjugate of \(1 + 2i\) is \(1 - 2i\).
  • (b) \(a = 1\), \(b = -6\), \(c = 13\).
Step 1: (a) Multiply by the conjugate

\[\frac{2 - 3i}{1 + 2i} \cdot \frac{1 - 2i}{1 - 2i} = \frac{2 - 4i - 3i + 6i^2}{1^2 + 2^2}\]

\[= \frac{2 - 7i - 6}{5}\]

\[= -\frac45 - \frac75 i\]

Step 2: (b) The discriminant

\[D = (-6)^2 - 4(1)(13) = 36 - 52 = -16 \lt 0\]

So there are no real solutions, but there are two complex ones.

Step 3: (b) The quadratic formula

\[x = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2} = \frac{6 \pm 4i}{2}\]

\[x = 3 \pm 2i\]

Answer

(a) \(-\dfrac45 - \dfrac75i\); (b) \(x = 3 + 2i\) and \(x = 3 - 2i\), a pair of complex conjugates.

Check

(a) \((1 + 2i)\left(-\tfrac45 - \tfrac75 i\right) = -\tfrac45 - \tfrac75 i - \tfrac85 i - \tfrac{14}{5}i^2 = -\tfrac45 + \tfrac{14}{5} - 3i = 2 - 3i\). ✓ (b) \((3 + 2i)^2 - 6(3 + 2i) + 13 = (5 + 12i) - 18 - 12i + 13 = 0\). ✓

Example 1.8 A rational inequality with a sign chart

Solve \(\dfrac{2x - 1}{x + 3} \le 1\). Write the answer in interval notation.

(SRW, Inequalities)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • A rational inequality; \(x \ne -3\).
  • Asked: the solution set.
Step 1: Move everything to one side

We do not multiply by \(x + 3\), since its sign is unknown.

\[\frac{2x - 1}{x + 3} - 1 \le 0\]

\[\frac{2x - 1 - (x + 3)}{x + 3} \le 0\]

\[\frac{x - 4}{x + 3} \le 0\]

Step 2: Cut points

The numerator is 0 at \(x = 4\) and the denominator is 0 at \(x = -3\). They give the intervals \((-\infty, -3)\), \((-3, 4)\), \((4, \infty)\).

Step 3: Sign chart

Test \(x = -4\): \(\tfrac{-8}{-1} \gt 0\). Test \(x = 0\): \(\tfrac{-4}{3} \lt 0\). Test \(x = 5\): \(\tfrac{1}{8} \gt 0\).

\[(-\infty, -3): +, \qquad (-3, 4): -, \qquad (4, \infty): +\]

Step 4: Endpoints

We want \(\le 0\), so \(x = 4\) (where the fraction is 0) is included. \(x = -3\) makes the denominator 0, so it is excluded.

Answer

\((-3, 4]\)

Check

At \(x = 0\): \(\tfrac{-1}{3} \le 1\) ✓. At \(x = -5\): \(\tfrac{-11}{-2} = 5.5 \le 1\) is false, so \(-5\) correctly lies outside. If we had multiplied by \(x + 3\) at the start, we would get \(x \le 4\) and wrongly include \(-5\).

Example 1.9 A PAGASA temperature forecast

PAGASA forecasts that tomorrow's temperature \(C\) in Manila (in °C) will satisfy \(|C - 30| \le 4\). A balikbayan relative asks for the range in °F. Use \(F = \tfrac95C + 32\).

(SRW, Inequalities: absolute-value inequalities)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(|C - 30| \le 4\): the temperature is within 4 °C of 30 °C.
  • \(F = \tfrac95 C + 32\).
  • Asked: the range of \(F\).
Step 1: Remove the absolute value

\(|x| \le c\) means \(-c \le x \le c\).

\[-4 \le C - 30 \le 4\]

\[26 \le C \le 34\]

Step 2: Multiply all parts by \(\tfrac95\)

Since \(\tfrac95 \gt 0\), the inequalities keep their direction.

\[46.8 \le \tfrac95 C \le 61.2\]

Step 3: Add 32 to all parts

\[78.8 \le \tfrac95 C + 32 \le 93.2\]

\[78.8 \le F \le 93.2\]

Answer

The temperature will be between 78.8 °F and 93.2 °F, that is, \(|F - 86| \le 7.2\).

Check

The centre 30 °C becomes \(\tfrac95(30) + 32 = 86\) °F, and the spread 4 °C becomes \(\tfrac95(4) = 7.2\) °F. Take note that the 32 shifts the centre but not the spread.

Example 1.10 A circle from a diameter

The points \(P(-1, -2)\) and \(Q(5, 6)\) are the endpoints of a diameter of a circle. Find the equation of the circle and its \(x\)-intercepts.

Example 1.11 A linear model for taxi fares

A Manila taxi charges a fixed flag-down amount plus a fixed rate per kilometre. A 4 km ride costs ₱104 and a 10 km ride costs ₱185. (a) Find the fare \(F\) as a linear function of the distance \(d\). (b) Interpret the slope and the intercept. (c) How far can you ride for ₱300?

(SRW, Lines: slope as rate of change)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Two data points \((d, F)\): \((4, 104)\) and \((10, 185)\).
  • Asked: \(F = md + b\), its meaning, and \(d\) when \(F = 300\).
Step 1: Slope

\[m = \frac{185 - 104}{10 - 4} = \frac{81}{6} = 13.5\]

Step 2: Point-slope form, then solve for \(F\)

\[F - 104 = 13.5(d - 4)\]

\[F = 13.5d - 54 + 104\]

\[F = 13.5d + 50\]

Step 3: Interpret

The slope 13.5 is the rate: ₱13.50 for each kilometre. The intercept 50 is the fare at \(d = 0\), the flag-down amount of ₱50.

Step 4: Budget of ₱300

\[13.5d + 50 \le 300\]

\[13.5d \le 250\]

\[d \le 18.5\overline{185} \approx 18.5\]

Answer

(a) \(F = 13.5d + 50\). (b) ₱13.50 per km plus a ₱50 flag-down. (c) Up to about 18.5 km.

Check

\(F(10) = 135 + 50 = 185\) ✓ and \(F(18.5) = 249.75 + 50 = 299.75 \le 300\) ✓.

Example 1.12 Solving graphically

(a) Solve \(4 - x^2 \ge x + 2\) graphically and confirm algebraically. (b) Use the zero method to estimate the real solution of \(x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0\) to two decimal places.

(SRW, Solving Equations and Inequalities Graphically)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • (a) \(f(x) = 4 - x^2\) (a downward parabola), \(g(x) = x + 2\) (a line).
  • (b) \(y = x^3 - 2x - 5\).
Step 1: (a) Find where the graphs meet

Graphing both, the parabola and the line cross twice. Setting them equal:

\[4 - x^2 = x + 2\]

\[x^2 + x - 2 = 0\]

\[(x + 2)(x - 1) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = -2,\; x = 1\]

The points are \((-2, 0)\) and \((1, 3)\).

Step 2: (a) Read the inequality from the graph

We want the parabola on or above the line. That happens between the crossings: at \(x = 0\), \(f(0) = 4 \ge g(0) = 2\) ✓.

Step 3: (b) Locate the zero

The graph of \(y = x^3 - 2x - 5\) crosses the \(x\)-axis once. Let's evaluate near the crossing:

\[y(2) = -1, \qquad y(2.1) \approx 0.061\]

\[y(2.09) \approx -0.051, \qquad y(2.095) \approx 0.0050\]

So the zero lies between 2.09 and 2.095.

Answer

(a) \([-2, 1]\). (b) \(x \approx 2.09\) (more precisely, 2.0946).

Check

(a) At \(x = 2\): \(4 - 4 = 0 \ge 4\) is false, so 2 is correctly excluded. (b) Since the zero is below 2.095, it rounds to 2.09, not 2.10.
